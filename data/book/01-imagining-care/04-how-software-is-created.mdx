When most tech founders are asked who their idol is, they incorrectly answer someone like Elon Musk, Steve Jobs, or Jeff Bezos.

The correct answer they should have provided is Nancy G. Leveson, a professor at MIT who has created a vast wealth of knowledge about how to prevent accidents. She’s the author of three books on the subject of safer computer design, one being “Safeware: A guide to preventing accidents and losses caused by technology”.

She succinctly sums up the majority of what is needed for software of care in her prologue to Safeware:

> “One obvious lesson is that most accidents are not the result of unknown scientific principles but rather of a failure to apply well-known, standard engineering practices. A second lesson is that accidents will not be prevented by technological fixes alone, but will require control of all aspects of the development and operation of the system.”

Imagine that creating a piece of social good software is juggling. One ball is defining the social good aspect that your product aims to produce, and amplify. Another ball is taking that social good and turning it into a high-fidelity software product that is safe and adoptable. Last ball is developing and designing an entity, like a non-profit or a startup, around that product that enables distribution and financial stability. Is it any wonder how most products fail to last, with all of those things in the air at the same time?

Where these realities start to play out is the software development lifecycle. **The software development lifecycle is the process of ideating, developing a product, programming it, and releasing it to the market in a repetitive cycle.** Your goal will be to start this process, and along the way create a greater entity from it, in the name of creating social good.

## Meet the Software Development Lifecycle

When two computers love each other very much… Wait, not that creation? Software is created when a person or organization has an idea that they want to come to life. There are 4 major parts of creating a software product. In most companies, all 4 of these things may be happening in concert, at any given time. For most software products still being maintained, a product is constantly in one or more of these states. The cycle repeats every time a new product, feature, change, or idea is ideated.

The ideal flow for a software product is that you

- ideate
- design
- develop
- release

After you release the product to the general market, you add two additional goals to the cycle.

- maintain
- grow

In a perfect world, a product gains traction. That traction provides resources, which the inventor uses to incubate more development, products, and bigger strategies. The end goal is to have a funnel of constant growth and reinforcement of the product that benefits the entity that owns the product, and achieves the outcome and goals set out for it.

The process of growing and maintaining a software product can be seen as a funnel of ideas and tasks. The top of your funnel is a multitude of ideas that will sustain and keep the product existing and relevant for years. The middle is the layers of prioritization, strategy, and vision that’s able to determine what is relevant and when to do it. At the bottom of the funnel is your product, being updated, maintained, and serving users.

All of the work within the funnel should align with the goals of the entity that oversees it. If the software product is being operated as an independent developer with little financial backing, their funnel may contain only low-cost and high impact features. If the operating entity is a large company, their funnel feeds directly into their company strategy and financial revenue projections, like an IPO or fundraising round.

This flow of constant tending to the product is known as the software development lifecycle, otherwise called the SDLC. As you run the cycle beyond your first release, you will also need to maintain the pre-existing code, infrastructure, assets, and more. If you have ever rubbed your stomach and patted your head at the same time, and experienced the mental anguish that multi-tasking brings, you have formally experienced a version of the software development lifecycle.

## Understanding the cost of care.

Software of care faces a unique problem, and one that may particularly eat at resource constrained inventors. Often, you aren’t able to skip on the processes that can enable development to move faster. The principles that are required to make software viable for social good can work against the systems that provide resources for funding that software. Most modern advice for early products and startups is to ship things fast, not think too hard about harm, and to try a lot of ideas and re-iterate quickly. For software of care, this can be harmful advice.

Most early software startups from the start do not have to think too much about gaining and keeping user trust, accessibility, privacy, or security. In fact, they cut down on all of those things to keep iterating fast, and to pour as many resources as they can into finding product market-fit. Even when they scale up, the focus can be on scaling and growth instead of creating a solid foundation for the product.

This is a unique burden that software for social good entities must bear early, but can also be a chance to build practices early on that will last for the entire duration of the lifecycle of the product. The risk tier of the product you build and how people will be affected by failure determines how fast and loose you can go.

The good news is that taking on those burdens earlier creates a deeper moat, and a better relationship with the users you want to serve. Delaying these crucial pieces is delaying developing a large part of the core of what makes your product trusted.

## Repeating threads.

Often, the same threads for developing software of care occur again and again in different ways throughout the development process. Getting familiar with them early on will show how they continually surface repeatedly, and create the fidelity that a strong social good product is known for.

#### Maximize care, minimize harm.

The biggest goal developing any software of care product is one goal with two sides. A social good product needs to maximize the amount of care given to a user, and minimize the harm a product may cause or experience.

#### Fortify critical paths.

Certain features and parts of the product are integral to the user’s experience, and to them receiving care. These are labeled critical paths, which will become the software equivalent of a ‘handle with care’ sticker on a box full of glass. Critical paths may receive additional processes throughout the lifecycle. This can look like completing a hazard analysis on them, evaluating them for adversarial usage, adding redundancy or other fail-safe mechanisms at the system design stage, and including additional testing for them during development. Critical paths are labeled as such, because they’re the primary candidate for maximizing care and minimizing harm.

#### Maintain contextual awareness.

Building software of care on the system design side is a lot of translating empathy for the user and understanding of their usage patterns into assumptions that work with the user’s situation, not against it.

These can be very obvious, like if a user is in an abusive situation, don’t save information in plain text. Or, if you’re building a tool for low-income households, work to manage the amount of networking calls to reduce the amount of cellular they may use. However, there’s some context that is hard to think about unless you have specific lived experience. For that, we’ll discuss some of these points where you need to be contextually aware in the following section.

What’s important to note is that system’s designs role is to encode empathy and care in an engineering context, and that improperly managing a user’s context can inadvertently contribute to harm in the most vulnerable of users.

Although this is not comprehensive of all areas that you may need to be contextually aware, hopefully it helps you build the skill to recognize when technical constraints can exacerbate situations in the physical world, and how to build around those.

#### Build for actuality.

Y**our goal for your product is to align it with reality as fast as you possibly can, to increase the chance it will be adopted and can find a greater life in the world.** A product that survives is one that is built with the world in consideration. This is both the case for the product being deployed, and for the entity and inventors deploying the product. The mark of a good entity and a good product is one that is as well-tuned to usability and functionality of the product and the processes that make it good.

Beyond any specific prescriptive solutions, it’s important that:

- Your product is built for the reality of the users who will adopt it.
- Your systems are built to be used, and not just made to be checked off on paper.

Processes, like doing root cause analyses or hazard reviews, will not be completed if they aren’t realistic. The most important lens to have about building systems and features is, “how can I make this realistically usable?”. A system that is not incentivized, whether an accessibility test or a new feature, is a system that will not be adopted.

#### Mitigate and manage adverse events.

Every software product has, and will experience failures. The most important thing for a social good product is being able to mitigate and manage them effectively. Adverse events are technical and non-technical events that a user may experience that negatively affect them, or make them trust the software less or not find value in it. An adverse event is anything that deters a software from being used by a motivated user.

The number one goal is to get the user through an ideal user session, so they can receive the care promised by the product. ## Software development, in motion.

Each phase in the lifecycle has its own importance and nuance as it comes to software of care.

A lot of the specifics of the SDLC for a given software product depends a lot on the context of the operating entity of the product.

Often, a lot of massive companies that have many different codebases and products create intensive logistical challenges. This is one of the ways where startups and smaller companies can develop an advantage compared to larger entities.

By having an easier, or thinner SDLC, they are able to ship features more nimbly and to outmaneuver companies that require more co-ordination and time to go through the process, despite them having more resources.

**Ideation**:

When you are initially developing a product, ideation is about finding the core creation mechanism for what you want to build. Often, a mental health app can start out as a panic attack guidance flow, or an app about resources can expand into a social service eligibility finder. Initial ideation is about discovering what you want to do, and where the first rock to climb is that is most likely to get you to the top of the mountain.

Initial ideation is often where the inventor should develop a core care methodology (or expand on the original methodology) about how they want the user to benefit from the solution, and how their ideal product would function.

This is also where they develop early relationships with users and stakeholders in the space to start refining and narrowing in on the effectiveness of their work. Ideation is complete when the inventor has finished ideating and removed biases and assumptions from their solution, and have researched it thoroughly enough to believe in a case for adoption and utility.

As users interact with your product, ideation will move into determine what new ideas, fixes, changes, or other structural changes are needed in the future. At this point, what ideation looks like will greatly depend on your resources, your operating entity, and how your product is doing in the market.

This is where you come up with a theoretical system, feature, fix, or major idea that is part of a software product. This step may repeat a number of times until it is locked in enough to proceed to the design stage. This step may also be where ideal users are brought in to validate the idea, and to prove it has demand.

This is the lightest and often easiest part to revise of the cycle, and so a majority of research and development is focused here to reduce issues that may come up further down the line. Things are often exciting and in-flux, leading to a number of core discoveries that shape everything down the road.

<Callout type="key" title="Ideation for software of care looks like">

- Integrating co-creation with users
- Researching the history and full context of the problem

</Callout>

**Design**: Where the requirements and system design are shaped. This is where data structures, interfaces and systems are created and tested against requirements. This step also may be re-iterated a bit to narrow in on the best approach, and where users confirm that the implementation of the idea meets their needs. Ideally, this is where all of the components that may block development are completed and preliminarily thought out. This includes system design (how you will build something with software components), and UI/UX interface design (how the user will interact with your software product).

Design is where you put your care methodology into practice, and work to the constraints of your actual user. You will get the chance here to genuinely encode consideration and thought for how your product meets the user in their world, and where effectiveness of the tool can live or die. Design is the point at which your beta user can interact with a prototyped version of your tool, and where you can refine assumptions and work to reduce harm and improve care further before programming.

<Callout type="key" title="Design for software of care looks like">

- Implementing human design principles
- Considering adversarial harm process

</Callout>

**Development**: This is where the design starts to actually be built, in terms of code, infrastructure, content, and more. Development will proceed to a stage where things are considered ‘done’, but will go through rounds of internal and external testing to confirm correct functionality.

Development is where you program the interfaces and system design as required, and work to enable harm reduction through specific programming and deployment techniques. Development also starts the process of testing the product with live users on the codebase. Depending on your resources, you may have several different environments or rounds of testing that you enable to ensure code quality before deploying.

Development often continues and is where you do last pass of final checks for quality assurance prior to sending to the public.

<Callout type="key" title="Development for software of care looks like">

- Adding accessibility testing to release checklists
- Implementing automated testing suites that cover critical paths in more depth

</Callout>

**Market Release**: Market release is where the software version is deployed, and made available to the general public. This is where you start to see the most beautiful and terrifying part of it all: seeing your product gain legs and exist in the world without your guiding hand.

This is also where the most magic regarding your product comes to life. You will hopefully encourage people in one form or another to use the tool, and they will start sending feedback and interacting with the product in a way that shows you what you did right, and what you didn’t. By taking this feedback and establishing cycles of communication with the user, you’re able to recalibrate your product and expand it over time.

<Callout type="key" title="Market Release for software of care looks like">

- Rolling out to a small group of users, or the entire user base
- Alpha testing features with a group of advisors and stakeholders prior to general release

</Callout>

**Maintenance**: Maintenance is keeping up with the day to day work of your software, like updating packages, fixing bugs, or testing it. This is often built into the cycle, but may have to take place outside of it. Maintenance is a great place to determine how well the lifecycle is serving the needs of the product and the entity that develops it. Maintenance can also be penetration testing the application, doing annual reviews, fixing additional emergency bugs that cannot wait for a release in the cycle, incident management, and more.

Doing maintenance correctly is a major chance to continually develop a relationship with your users, and to prove yourself as a good steward of your software product.

<Callout type="key" title="Maintenance for software of care looks like">

- Sending update emails out to users
- Reviewing logs to find bugs and work to address them
- Look at analytics coming through of product usage

</Callout>

**Grow**: The purpose of most pieces of software is to get users, take feedback from them, and change the software to be the best product it can be. How far each piece of software grows depends on the operating entity which owns it. Some software products will not have growth as a goal, but the majority will.

Growth is where you get the chance to use your product in new ways, for new people, and potentially on new issues. When managed well, it becomes a lever to increase your impact and to further the amount of positive work you can do.

<Callout type="key" title="Growth for software of care looks like">

- Identifying other problems or groups that are viable beneficiaries of the product
- Making tradeoffs of impact vs. scale

</Callout>

**Evaluate**: Evaluation is the phrase in a product’s life where it’s tested to see how close it creates the impact it was originally designed to have.

<Callout type="key" title="Evaluation for software of care looks like">

- Completing randomized control trials (RCTs)
- Sending out surveys to users
- Reviewing outcome data

</Callout>
