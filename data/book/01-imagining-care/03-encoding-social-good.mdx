Software is undeniably ubiquitous, and there seems to be one (or many products) for any given use case. But, there’s a certain parts of it that make it a uniquely compelling form of care for specific use cases within a social good context. There’s also some important distinctions that can make software incorrect for solving a problem, or that can cause misplaced confidence in a solution that won’t survive coming to market.

## Benefits of a software product.

[Add fluff about benefits here]

### It can be easier to access.

The clearest answer for why software can be the best to deliver care is that it’s accessible to a large number of people. It can be cheap to operate compared to its vast scale, and software is great at doing standardized things very fast, for a big group. This gives it a unique value proposition that is not often able to be leveraged through in-person programs.

### It can be more standardized.

Software and the processes of releasing it can also be automated over time, enabling strong consistency, standardized experiences, and productivity gains that in-person programs would struggle to match. In addition, it can be easier to access and find in times of crisis or immediate need, and can often transcend places where humans might not be able to, or not want others to, go.

### It can be more trusted.

A strong product is able to facilitate a type of intimacy that is rare with other humans. Psychologists call the human equivalent the “stranger on a train” effect, where people can be most honest and disclose the most to people who have no stakes in their life, or no chance of showing up again[^4]. Software makes this fleeting and uncommon dynamic available at scale, when the user needs it.

A majority of people have experienced a scary situation they experienced where their first impulse was to go to the internet for answers. As early as 2011, 79% of people looked for health information online, with ease of access and anonymity being primary reasons [0]. The intimacy provided by software comes from the fact it can feel so generalized and anonymous, while also not spilling into the user’s life by requiring another person to know about the situation at hand. This digital intimacy enables apps and websites to be able to tend to people in situations where they otherwise would be entirely shut off.

The anonymity and vast access can also enable people to admit situations prior to bringing them up with other people in their social orbit. To go to your community and say you think you are experiencing domestic violence can be scary, shameful, and hard to swallow. To explore definitions of what counts as domestic violence, read the experience of others (and see yourself in them), and to know what resources are available can push someone to take a first step they wouldn’t have otherwise considered. This is especially important for stigmatized communities, or people going through stigmatizing issues.

In research, anonymity has a positive correlation with online disclosure, enabling users to somewhat shed their inhibitions and get their innate needs met. Face to face disclosure can carry legitimate social costs, ones that are less likely with software.[^2][^3]

This is one reason why hotlines are uniquely situated to help those in crisis. The combination of anonymity, speed to access, and guarantee of confidentiality and availability can provide the circumstances for someone to reach out and say things they could never say otherwise.

### It can be cheaper to deliver care.

Software is uniquely positioned in terms of unit economics, especially when it comes to serving vast amounts of people at scale. It is relatively cheap to build initially, and can reach to a high number of people in a way that no other physical program can.

The volume of people using the product can enable strong negotiating leverage, and many bigger products benefit from volume pricing. Software companies are so often the beneficiaries of venture capital spend because the profit margins of software can be one of the best in business.

A software product is easily made accessible for a diverse group of people in a way that other assets are not. For example, an app can easily be translated into English, Chinese, and Spanish. But, brochures at a local health clinic may need 3 separate copies that need to be continuously stored and otherwise maintained. Even then, the internationalization of software is easier to do logistically and procedurally in many ways. Translation across different languages is handled automatically by many front-end frameworks, and accessibility is more easily built into a software product than it is to make a physical location ADA compliant.

Software also has less limits on distribution apart from the bottleneck of infrastructure, in the same way an in person program does. It does not have the same constraints as in-person programs would, nor the rent requirements, personnel needs, or parking spaces. This lack of physicality and leverage of ubiquitousness is its greatest strength and biggest weakness.

The actual cost of developing and deploying a software product can range from incredibly simple with a $20/month Squarespace subscription to serve thousands, or can be as high as $10-50 per user a month in infrastructure and vendor costs for a low-volume, high compliance needs software product.

The cost of software depends ultimately on who is using it, how many people are using it, and the usage patterns necessitated by the product. Software is most cost efficient with low-complexity, low requirement, and high scale users. Generally, the more requirements, complexity, and constraints placed on a product, the more expensive it will be.

## Negatives of a software product.

### It can be a mirage of impact.

The ease of creating software can be a mirage: software is easy to create but hard to maintain and invest in over the long term. It is easy to deploy a product and call it innovative, and much harder to create the entity and circumstances that allow that innovation to be adopted and diffused through a mass of people that would enable a good return on investment.

This seeming ease to start often trips up non-profits or smaller social good programs who assume they only need to convert their website into an app and reap the benefits. This is, in many instances, not the case. Often, niche in-person programs don’t have a big enough audience or value proposition that could take full advantage of the scale or access derived from a software solution.

Software can be easy to make and has a lower bar for entry, which means it’s often competing with a vast number of other products. Humans can also have shorter attention spans for software products, which makes the impact from software potentially lessened. It also makes the moat that software needs to be greater, as physical territory and local support are no longer defensible spaces to lean on. A piece of software someone uses is not competing within its’ singular category, it is competing against every other piece of software they have, or have given their email or other marketing distribution point to.

### It may not be the right solution.

Many problems can be addressed with a piece of software, but if they should be can be an entirely different equation. The underlying determination is the rationale behind building a product. Building a software product is entirely different from building a strong online presence. Even more so, the allure that technology can change the world and automatically accelerate impact is not true. If you are reading this book, and not on a yacht, it is likely not true for you either.

Many people want to build software because of its’ associations with power, scale, innovation, and prestige. Very few of those things ever come to fruition, even in regards to general for-profit products. A non-profit organization having an app does not automatically mean it has innovated, or is tech-adept. On the other side, a non-profit that utilizes an app from the ground up as a core part of delivering on its’ mission could likely say the same.

All of that to say: software being the solution depends on what exactly the problem is. We will discuss later in the chapter what entities will, and will not benefit from a product.

## Software, in our world.

### Inventors, and their products.

People’s backgrounds for coming to build social good software can be incredibly varied. The most consequential person in a software’s development is it’s inventor, the person who defines the product and comes to create the initial feature set and bring it to market. These nuances will go to predict problems with the product ahead of time, which can help inform what kind of education and direction you may want to take on.

| **Inventor Background** | **Positive Aspects** | **Negative Aspects** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Tech Background* | Openness to discussing new ideas, experimentation, orientation towards innovation | Overestimating the power of technology, not understanding the dynamics at play in a social good context |
| *Non-profit* | Ability to navigate social good spaces, network | Not understanding technology and how to correctly or wisely implement it |
| *For-profit VC* | Understanding of business sustainability, clarity around pre-existing markets and context | Lack of hands-on experience building a business, potential distance from understanding social good programs and what they take to operate. |
| *Repeat Founder* | Ability to execute, experience in regards to problem solving, network to call on. | Overreliance on previous experience, ‘know it all’ syndrome, locked into biases |
| *First-time Founder* | Exuberance and energy, vision and passion. | High learning curve, lack of network to rely on to support product diffusion |

### The interplay of in-person and digital programs.

Increasingly, a number of companies are employing a dual hybrid model of operating an in-person program or service business or program, and having a digital layer as a complimentary solution. When done correctly, there can be a strongly diversified combination that leverages in-person and digital to their best attributes. When done wrong, the efforts of either side of the business can suffer, and lack of focus and resources can stunt the entity.

Software as a delivery mechanism, in my opinion, is best suited for more broad social good applications or applications where accessibility to those who can use devices is a primary consideration. There may also be applications where social good entities strongly benefit from both an in person presence, and a digital software product.

In these dual instances, software should be viewed as less of a replacement for in-person services, and more an additional layer of access. The goal is to enable both distribution points (in-person & digital) to focus on what they excel at and serve complimentary, yet separate use cases.

The best examples of programs that benefit from both are national programs that have a clear analogy into the software space. For example, Alcohol’s Anonymous has in-person meeting groups and video call only chapters. They benefit from an app because the service is analogous online and offline, and they are providing complimentary services from the same care methodology. They will also reach a threshold of scale where the investment and maintenance of building software will be repaid through the impact they would gain.

Having dual programs can be a strain on entities that may struggle with funding, or do not have the resources or brand power to pull together a strong technical team interested in the project. The scale a hybrid program sometimes necessitates can require specialized infrastructure engineers and a higher attention to load, as well as remodeling the data of legacy systems that may power the in-person programs.

Dual programs are often best done by legacy non-profits or institutions, or programs that have a high degree of resources and technical expertise that can traverse problems that plague software development.

### Where can software do good?

This is meant to be a general guide, and not an exhaustive list. Like everything, there are certainly use cases and examples that may not align. But, this is a good short list of deciding where software is best applied to social good issues.

**Social good programs that would benefit from being software-only or software-enabled are:**

- Are broad, but niche enough to garner attention and develop a moat on
- Benefit from a national or international audience
- That have a clear digital counterpart in a way that is more efficient than an in person service.
- Require discretion, or have massive amounts of stigma associated with them to the point where in-person service is a deterrent to help.
- Need a high degree of consistency
- Serve those with digitally compatible accessibility needs
- Can develop clear and sustainable moats, with a competitive value proposition.
- Have the capacity to properly care for and invest in the software solutions they want to bring to life

**Programs that may not benefit from a software product are where:**

- The entity is not able to commit to maintaining and investing in a software product in a sustainable way (like a non-profit that has initial development budget, but not additional year over year maintenance budget)
- The potential impact and benefit are small, and to a niche group of users
- The service provided doesn’t have a strong advantage given by digital distribution, or compared to other offerings in the market
- Have a lot of customizations within their program offerings that require large variations or configurations.
- The end user could be at a disadvantage because of lack of access, disability, or technical literacy (like elderly populations, unhoused users, etc).
- The primary benefit is the in-person experience, and the socialization and community aspect of care.

## Why software of care fails.

Unfortunately, most software of care products statistically fail. This is not limited to social good software, but is the reality of most new products. Understanding why and how they fail prior to starting your own can help understand some of the pitfalls that may trap you.

Although using pre-existing forms of care is a strong starting point for your product, even adapting in-person services or pre-existing care to digital software products can fail. This adaption curve can be a big struggle when determining the effectiveness and impact of your solution, even if the research it is initially based on can *technically* be correct.

This can also be why non-technical entities and inventors may be less successful than technical entities and inventors, as accounting for this digital curve can be more intuitive if you have been through it before.

### A lot of social good is human connection.

A human has a different relationship to a piece of software with distinct social rules, separate from the relationship a human would have with another human. This failure to account for new social rules can result in products that look primed for impact on the surface, but do not make the connections in practice that enable the care methodology to work as intended.

Although a service that occurs in person may be highly successful and seems ready for a digital addition or replacement, it can be the experience of someone in and around a community or supportive person, and not the process itself.

Research has showed that one of the strongest predicators of success in therapy is the connection the therapist shares with the patient.[^5] Technique specific factors account for a smaller fraction of positive outcomes. The human bond, agreement and accountability on goals, and trust built between the two parties account for a much higher percentage[^6]. What this means in laymen’s terms is that the relational aspect of therapy can be just as effective, if not more, than the actual therapeutic modality underlying the sessions.

If you were to just take those flows without the human connection, you would lose a major driver of positive outcomes. Therapy with a person enables accountability outside of someone’s social circle. The patient is also being held accountable to another person, who they can be worried about disappointing or making upset. The patient is also paying money and devoting clear effort to have that relationship, leading them to have skin in the game. That relational context does not translate to the realm of software.

A user who engages with a mental health app does not have a relationship with a human: it has a relationship with an interface. They may not worry about disappointing this interface, and the experience of interacting with this flow of CBT prompts is not similar to the experience of interacting with a human who is asking the same questions. Software that faces this problem will need to find a different mechanism that is able to make up for, or replace the human analog.

I’ll bring you to an example that was the bane of my career for about 2 years. Faura is a company that I cofounded in 2023 to build software that estimates the resilience of a home to a natural disaster.

When we got into the space, the standard was in-person inspections of a home by a certified inspector. These inspectors, depending on the natural disaster, were done by a structural engineer, defensible space inspector, or home inspector. They often required someone showing up and poking around, or were done by someone driving past the property without the homeowner’s knowledge on behalf of a local government or insurer.

Insurance companies, seeing major losses from natural disasters, were moving towards a property level focus to determine how prepared each home was. Instead of relying on a NATCAT model to determine if a home was in a high-risk area, they were looking at each home within a high-risk area to see if it would withstand a natural disaster when one occurred. Most applications tackling this issue were simply digitizing these resilience inspections, and selling the inspection data to insurance companies.

As such, we also embarked on creating a web app equivalent of in-person NATCAT inspections. Homeowners and customers seemed to like it because it was a clear 1-to-1 of the solution in the space, just made digital and easier in some ways. That initial acceptance was actually a detriment to us, as it wasn’t until further development was completed that the product failed to land in its’ stated go-to-market capacity. What seemed like market validation was actually a vague nod of recognition to a previously adopted solution, but not our solution as it stood as a product.

Our web-app reviewed a homeowner’s property related to wildfire, wind, hail, flood, ice dam, and earthquake. We would pre-fill information about the property from aerial images and public records to reduce the amount of work needed. The homeowner would complete the initial survey and verify answers, and we would provide a customized resilience report to the homeowner and insurer about the state of the property.

We operated the product from the mindset that we needed to be extremely thorough in collecting property characteristics. This often meant asking homeowners to take photos of, and look for things they had never thought about or categorized in the first place. But then, we rolled out to insurance companies and homeowners. They didn’t hate it, but they didn’t embrace it in the way we thought they would.

The amount of questions we were asking to homeowners, and photo validation we required to ensure correctness of their answers, was a big blocker for both homeowners and insurers. Insurers also didn’t like the long time a digital inspection process could take, and thought it was too similar to regular home inspection applications in the market to buy as an additional product. It also didn’t truly serve the core need for resilience offerings in the space, which was more suited to complying with regulation and ensuring transparency as opposed to getting the highest fidelity data capture from the homeowner.

We then pivoted to a different concept: scoring the property without the homeowner’s input, and then emailing them a report and asking them to correct information they didn’t think was right. Engagement shot up, and insurers we worked with reported record levels of engagement with their pilots. Instead of asking homeowners to pre-fill all information, similar to a standard inspection, we just showed them what we had.

Them realizing information their insurer had was incorrect was a strong enough motivator to get them to engage. We then situated our property self assessment (or PSA) as the last step in the funnel instead of the first. Homeowners now only go through the PSA when they are applying for discounts on their property or proving compliance with mitigation standards.

So, although in-person inspections were a good idea as an in-person service, they did not have a clear digital analogue. It was only by building a digital-first approach to resilience that respected the user’s lack of time and situational cues that we were able to build something impactful.

### User interfaces and features can be interchangeable.

Software products can be indistinguishable from others, and it can be a struggle for users to strongly be attached to or remember a piece of software, even if it’s meant to help them. A lot of reasons create this seeming abundance of software from an end-user perspective:

- Modern UI/UX standards create very consistent web pages for usability, and this usability reduces novelty in exchange for ease of onboarding and utility.
- Software is being developed at a pace unlike any other time in history, and there are more applications and products available for download than there ever was.
- People use software to do check-ins at their doctor’s office, or to order their coffee or groceries. The ubiquity and expectation of software as an offering can pressure companies to develop solutions. It also is viewed as a competitive advantage, way to drive customer loyalty, and to own a distribution channel.
- Many software companies adopt features that their competitors have to make themselves level in the market, leading to many products in the space narrowing in on the same feature set once major market adoption has shown promise.

The combination of the above is why software companies often pour so much money into developing their brand, features, and engagement methods. It is also why, even if you go to the deep dark lengths to develop a piece of software, distribution, scale, and longevity are the true challenges ahead in the future. Providing care for a user or having well-wishes is a good start, but it is not enough alone to create a product that stands out and lasts.

Products that are poorly designed, incorrectly sized, or just too cumbersome will not be adopted by users. As digital products become more common, and less valuable, users are more and more accustomed to highly detailed and interactive software that is easy to use and developed by companies with millions, or billions of dollars.

It is often hard to compete with that, especially as a resource constrained startup, nonprofit, or solo venture. Software products that are developed by a non-technical entity or inventor may suffer from this issue.

### A product may fail to latch into situational cues.

Every time a user opens up their phone or their email, a software product will not just be competing with the competitors in their domain: it will be competing with every app that sends push notifications, or every company that has access to that user’s email. You are standing out against tens, hundreds, or thousands of calls to action. Users are bombarded with calls for attention consistently, and most of these calls are ignored.

To be effective, software often needs to be linked into a timed component of a user’s life. This is also why the value proposition you need to have must be incredibly strong, and emotionally tied to a situational cue in the eyes of the user.

If a software fails to latch into a situational cue, it can fail to become usable in a constant way. For some software products, like those that provide resources or act as an information hub, this may not be a concern given it’s not the primary goal. But for the majority of software products that need retention to survive, failing to tie into a consistent enough situational cue can mean a product is used once or twice before being forgotten, and fading into the background of the attention landscape.

Even if the product is able to latch onto a situational cue, it may also not be a wise idea to remind the user. When I was building a security system app, a primary consideration was the inability to send calls to action to the user reminding them that this product was around if they needed it. Sending out a call for attention could also draw an abuser’s attention, and cause more harm to the user for having a safety mechanism in place.

Another way I’ve experienced the pain of situational cues is at Faura, where we provide action items to homeowners. Often, they are not in a position to be able to do this work when we first give them the report. Depending on the customer setup, we often provide it at initial binding of the policy (when someone first applies for insurance), at renewal (when the insurance policy is reviewed and renewed or cancelled), or in concert with a major NATCAT season (like in the summer before wildfires or hurricanes start to occur).

Therefore, we’ve developed products to time the report generation towards specific seasons and events. Then, we are giving people advice during fire season, or as a hurricane is predicted to touch down near them in 4-5 days, not when they are receiving this information for the first time and managing multiple other things in their life.

Situational cues and being able to leverage them depends highly on the context of your user. If you do not have a clear hook into situational cues, you must find an alternate path or cue to hook into, or a way to respect a user’s circumstance without disappearing. It is not always about providing information when it is most advantageous to you, but is most advantageous and actionable to the end user.

### Software companies may fail to gain trust.

Users can be increasingly distrustful of software companies. Although this kind of mindset is common throughout the world, this is especially pronounced in vulnerable communities who may be the subjects of surveillance, harm, or experienced negative effects of the adoption of technology.

If you have been looking at how software has entangled its’ way through world, this may not be surprising to you. Companies often break the trust of users, or fail to ever gather it in the first place. Many software projects are announced that promise to change the lives of people facing an issue, but without ever interacting with those people affected or researching the problem they’ve addressed in depth.

Further, products have been the source of immense harm to some communities. Data breaches, misinformation, surveillance, denial of access to resources through automatic health insurance claim rejections or mortgage application rejections, and other countless forms of technologies belie access. These experiences shape technology not only as a source of innovation, but another way to extract and utilize data on behalf of a faceless system that does not provide paths for remediation when it fails.

It is important to know and to work around these issues before a product comes to face them, and to let users’ perceptions shape a product and business model. For example, people do not always trust period tracker apps knowing that the data could be used to determine pregnancy or other medical problems. Therefore, anyone building a non-profit period tracking or pregnancy management application would be wise to avoid selling that data to a data broker, or sharing it with any 3rd parties at all.

Your assignment is to not just build a product that solves a solution, but to build a product worth trusting. When you are aiming to impact a user’s life, you are going to be scrutinized and critiqued, as you rightly should be. Learning to build that level of transparency and due diligence throughout your product development will make the choice to choose your product a much easier one.

—

Software is a different landscape where not every flower may bloom to its full potential. Although you should base your care on the most evidence-based foundation that you can, you will need to build your own form of measurement to make sure the impact intended actually translates to the end user - regardless of how strong the initial case for impact is prior to development.

The best software of care is the kind that utilizes the benefits of software to its fullest advantage, in a way that in-person services cannot. Prior to engaging in developing software from a base of evidence, ideally you will understand what makes that method of care effective and how it will directly translate to software that they are able to create.

## Why software of care succeeds.

Software that succeeds in fulfilling its’ goal, generally is very good at what it does. That sounds simple on the surface, but reveals a large amount of work, fore-planning, timing, and other edges of detail that go into building something that meets the mission it has set out for itself.

### A product integrally meets a need.

The product has done its’ due diligence enough to determine a need or aspiration of the community that faces a problem. Even more so, hopefully it takes advantage of human factors that further its’ adoption. It is not solving a problem that doesn’t exist, but is solving one that does. A good product is built almost anthropoligized around the life of its’ user segments.

### The operators are well-resourced.

The entity that creates, maintains, and grows the software is properly resourced. Not just in financial aspects (although that is a core piece of the puzzle), but in terms of will, personnel, network, ambition, and empathy. A product is not just created and sent into the world imagining that its’ utility will uniquely drive adoption: it’s created and given the best chance and effort the operating entity has at introducing it into the world. This can also be a double edged sword: products that have little to no efficacy can also be backed by intense amounts of resources or with the support of pre-existing solutions. Then, ‘phantom care’ products can walk the Earth, taking mainstream resources and appearing as a solution to a problem yet unsolved. Spooky indeed.

### The functionality is user-oriented and driven.

The tooling of the application is derived from the needs and aspirations of a user as they face a problem. A good product orients less around what is convenient for the operating entity, and makes decisions based on what is best accessible and formatted to the user. User feedback and their opinions are treated as as first class citizen, and the product is built and oriented around situational awareness for the user.

### It meets its’ problem in a unique manor.

The product works and leverages its’ unique advantages, instead of working to build new advantages while developing the product.

### It spreads through stakeholders.

This does not apply for every product, but products with longevity and momentum usually pair with stakeholders as best as they can.

### Digital mechanisms are leveraged intently.

A software product that works has a unique digital consideration that becomes a defining mechanism of interacting with the product. Going back to the ‘strangers on the train’, a text line or heath information app is unique because it leverages digital tools to create intimacy. Tools

### It’s built with vibrant consideration.

Providing care is about detail. The same is true for scaled, social good products. If you have a wife, and yet you do not pay attention to the details or tend to her needs accurately or properly over time, what do you have? Divorce proceedings. The hardest part of building a product is not coming up with the idea, but developing a product around it, systematizing it, and bringing it to fruition while keeping intact that vibrant consideration.
