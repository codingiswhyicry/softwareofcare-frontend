In the rooms of memory care facilities across the world, patients with dementia and Alzheimer’s coo lovingly and pet a harp seal. Or, what they believe to be a pet seal. It’s actually a $6,000 robot that does not live in water, PARO.

PARO interacts with people in long-term care settings to increase their comfort, and reduce their stress and pain. The fuzzy friend’s user manual is also quite endearing. If PARO is upset, you are instructed to pet it gently until it calms down. When PARO is sleepy, you charge it by putting the “pacifier” in its’ mouth. Both steps seem reasonable.

PARO was initially designed for use in Dementia and Alzheimer’s patients, but almost 30 years after its’ creation, it continues to be studied and utilized in new formats, and for new treatments. A recent study shows that PARO was able to reduce the experience of pain, and their improve mood in multiple parties.

This beloved seal imposter is a great example of what it looks like to encode care into a product. By being a distraction and a physical entity that requires care and provides affection, PARO provides research-proven care to its’ users (and delight to me).

—-

Providing care has been crucial for enabling survival and expansion among humanity. Care is crucial to our wellbeing, society, and our overall success as individuals, communities, and societies. Because of its’ power, it is often misused, or cloaked in many layers of nuance and history that can prevent it from occurring fully.

Although we need it to survive and become abundant, providing care is deeply nuanced from person to person, background to background, and problem to problem. Simply put, ask 100 different people how they want to be cared for, and you will most likely have 100 different variations of answers.

A defining constraint of software for social good is being able to develop a standardized form of care across variations of people that is effective enough for each individual. Developing a product that is too broad can make it fail to be effective among individuals. But, in developing a product that is too niche, it can fail to be a scalable product to larger markets, negating the digital benefits it has.

## Deliberating on bias.

Humanity’s ideas about what help is will inherently drive the biases in the software we create. Someone's idea of help for those in poverty might be to establish financial literacy courses; another's might be to build a micro-loan application. Each of these ideas engrains a diagnosis: a financial literacy tool assumes the problem is knowledge, whereas a micro-loan app assumes the problem is capital.

One of the primary issues with defining and building effective software of care is the amount of bias and politicization about what “care” for a user means.

The solution we reach for reveals what we believe causes and solves the suffering. The end product built is a reflection of our diagnosis of the issue, which is tinted politically by nature. Political ideas about crime, welfare, poverty, race, sexuality, disability (and more) shape the solutions that are brought to the communities that interface with those issues.

The end result means we bring half-baked ideas formed in political arenas *to* communities, instead of solutions created in unison *with* those communities. The co-creation needed to build a truly impactful product is needed from the origin of the idea, to the end execution of sending a product to market. Co-creation is almost a necessity for adoption: people do not want care that they do not trust, made by people they feel like have no true skin in the game, and lack of co-creation amplifies the red flags that makes users not trust a product.

Start by asking yourself: **what you want the end goal of your care to be?** Do you want your care to make people feel less alone, therefore increasing the quality of life? Do you want them to access tools that make hardships easier, therefore easing the strain of what they go through?

It’s important to understand the flaws in a diagnosis prior to building them into a product. Biases are blinding because they can tunnel-vision inventors into a solution that may not be the most effective for the problem they are working to solve. In that sense, removing your biases is a way to double check your work, do due-diligence on behalf of your users, and insure it’s effective from the root: you.

Understanding a problem is a mental model that you develop and understand how you interact within. One perspective is just a layer, and gathering as many perspectives on the problem is how you truly understand what you are aiming to get into. For a product to truly care for people, it needs to be nuanced and thought out beyond your initial belief or gut feeling about a problem. This is true even if you have experience in an industry, or have built a solution within the space prior.

Every solution is a prism that can enable so many angles, and ways to interpret it. Work to develop out the prism of your solution as much as you can, and view it from as many angles as you can manage.

There is a great amount of literature out there about social service programs and what is actually effective long term. Campbell Collaboration is a great resource that peer reviews non-profit programs. The Cochrane library is another great resource that focuses specifically on healthcare, and What Works Clearinghouse focuses specifically on education programs.

As someone working to do this big effort to help people, I would imagine that you are greatly invested in doing it correctly and giving it the best shot you can. The products that humans build start from within, and how we feel about the problem we are approaching.

You have to be willing to be wrong, and to go in with an open mind to truly enable yourself to find how to give care. In foregoing your own sense of self, you may find something much greater: a clear arc in the sky showing you how to help people that you really want to help. It is a hard, and uncomfortable thing to work to hold your own judgements back. But, it’s an early chance to grow alongside the product, and to create an invention greater than what you are.

## Miscalibration of care.

Care is a thing that carries some level of societal status if you provide it, and can act as a tarp on a broken roof, that makes people overlook negative or ineffective action. Because it is not leaking, we do not replace the tarp, nor fix the roof. The roof isn’t really fixed, but we learn to live with the gaping hole in the sky instead of fixing it.

This is why it’s important to not just accept “help” or “care” on its face, but to investigate through research, user interviews, and supposed evidence to determine what the true outcome is.

**1. Care doesn’t enable autonomy**. Software meant to help without the effort of correctly calibrating that help to the end mindset of the user, or without consideration for their needs, can come across as condescending, or paternalizing. A good inventor proactively gets ahead of this as early as they can, as it poisons the well of a product over time. Paternalization or savior complex stems from the belief that you know what is better for someone than what they believe to be good for them, or that you in some way are better or more advanced than them and are able to provide care. The primary issues with such beliefs is that they create uncomfortable power dynamics for the person receiving care, and are dehumanizing. They also illuminate how far the gap is between the person giving and the person receiving care is. People do not want to feel like a charity case, or as though they are being set on a lower rung of the ladder for you to look down at them from above.

They want to feel as though you are a true co-conspirator and person working to help them from an equal relationship. It is in developing this respect for your users, and working to level yourself to them that you set yourself up for truly enabling care.

**2. Care doesn’t have co-ordination.** Even though they have passion about what they speak, if you don’t fundamentally agree on the premise of care someone wants to give to you, you won’t accept that care. Same for your users. You can spend millions developing a perfect application, and it means nothing without buy-in from your user. That buy-in starts with research, and relationships built from understanding.

In that way, the mindset of how you view giving care, what it enables for you to do, and how someone should receive it sets the stage for your relationship to the problem and user.

**3. Care is not derived from evidence.** Abuse can be rebranded as care. Many programs have recast humiliation and labor as "treatment," enforcing their idea of healing through pain. This is especially common in criminal justice, substance abuse, and mental health programs.

These abusive programs are a way for companies to extract cheap labor or other benefits from people who cannot refuse. The pain they claim as a method is the cover that lets them operate in the dark. Ask the question their marketing will never: does it actually improve outcomes for the person going through it? Often, the evidence will say no.

People can be convinced anything can be helpful. That is why it is so important, perhaps more important than anything else you do, to establish evidence for your product being beneficial, and put effort into designing a research backed care methodology. You need to know that what you are advancing and sending into the world at scale is truly beneficial, and doesn’t just ‘feel’ like it. Testing your assumptions and biases to align with research backed evidence, and remove your own first opinions from what care, helps to create a new definition of what care could be.

## Calibration of care.

It feels clear to understand what kind of care people don’t want. But understanding what kind of care people do want feels less intuitive. But truly, most people have the same desires for recognition, autonomy, and care that everyone alive does. Formatting that and building it into the product development process will go a long way towards sweetening the relationships that enable a product to be there for people in their darkest moments.

- **People want to be advised and feel like they're contributing.** There's a difference between being helped and being handled for someone else’s agenda. People contribute back to products that make room for their contribution. They want to see feedback that visibly changes things, choices that are genuinely theirs, because contributing is proof they're still the author of their own situation.
- **They want to feel like they will benefit from the solution.** The benefit someone gets from your product should be theirs, on their terms, and visible early. A product that promises a payoff downstream, after the forms, onboarding, or behavior change, can be asking for faith from people whose faith has usually been spent by bad actors prior to you. Someone’s honest calculation about deciding to use your product may boil down to: “what am I getting, and what is it costing me?" A solid product will help the user will survive that math, without needing to be defended.
- **They want to be treated with respect.** Respect in a product is mostly the absence of small insults: no jargon that assumes stupidity, no tone that assumes fragility, nor design that assumes bad faith. It shows up in defaults that trust people, error messages that don't scold, and eligibility flows that don't treat every applicant as a suspected fraud. People in hard circumstances are usually experts on those circumstances, and respect means the product acts like it knows that.
- **They want to feel like they are not having to do all of the work to make a product work.** People who are the beneficiaries of a product should be engaging because they want their work to be included, but not because they don’t think anyone else is doing the work that needs to be done. Be wary of over leveraging key people who
- **They want to feel as though their input is a function of the system and not a checkbox.** Your users are smart people, and they will know the difference between feedback that steers and feedback that decorates. A survey whose results never surface again teaches users their voice is ornamental, and they stop offering it. Working to prioritize engaging your user for the sake of actual development and for granting them autonomy into something they use will go far better than engaging your user for the sake of saying you have an engaged user base.
- **They want to feel like they're gaining a partner who's aligned with their goals, and not being preached at.** Users can determine the agenda within moments of opening a product, and everything else flows downstream of what they detect.

The difference between care people want and care people don't often comes down to whose agenda is being served. Imagine a teacher who stays after class to help you pull a grade up because they know you're aiming for college to go build rockets, because it’s your childhood dream. You told that teacher about the fact your dad would live-stream Space Shuttle launches for you, and they remember that and use it to develop out the version of you that YOU want to be. The teacher's help serves your goal: they've noticed what you're trying to do, they meet you inside your effort, and their success is defined by yours.

Conversely, think of religious missionaries knocking on the door. However sincere, the interaction starts from their agenda. you are the target of a plan you didn't ask to be part of, most likely did not request, and the "care" on offer moves you along their map. Both parties would describe themselves as helping, but only one of them asked where you want to go.

The teacher's version of care makes you a contributor, respects your direction, and does the work of showing up inside your existing goals. The missionary version arrives with the answer preformed, and your role is to receive it willingly. Users can tell within moments which kind of product they're holding, and they extend or withhold trust accordingly.

## Giving care people want.

It’s easy to pontificate about the care that people don’t want, and what makes them upset. It’s harder further, to define the care that people want and how to capture that within a product. The easiest way to figure out what is care that people want is to ask them and watch their behavior. Beyond that, there’s set rules that you should follow through on to establish a good relationship that lets you see into these inner worlds to define what good care is.

There are often pre-existing ways that people care for each other and themselves, and being able to observe and see how care is already shaped is a great indicator of what would be a natural extension of that care. Remittance software is a great example of what this looks like. Immigrant communities often send money back home, and well-done remittance software with low fees and clear interfaces are a strong example of software of care, by embedding something the community already did and making it easier and more consistent.

Each of these principles will be unsurprising, and quite boring: that’s unfortunately the point. People want to be treated with respect, care, kindness, and as a co-builder in their future instead of being treated like a mal-aligned advisor in a product that someone else wants to feed them. These principles also stack up on each other, and are a natural progression of building a relationship with someone else.

- **They want to feel like you’ve done your research**

First and foremost, people often want to see that you are motivated to do this work and to understand them. One of the best ways to do so and to show consideration is to do a strong amount of research, and then to use others to add context and provide their perspective.

- **They want to know that you understand their needs / aspirations**

People want to work with people who feel aligned with them and what they want. A lot of rifts in the social good space can be summarized as a difference between the treatment or options that someone wants for themselves, and what someone is able to give them. Especially when people are going through the hard things that a social good program aims to solve, emotions run high and people can often feel misunderstood and neglected. Every person has their own aspirations and hopes, and people do things to align with those aspirations.

- **People want to know that you’re aligned with their goals**

This is the biggest one, and the one that most outsiders struggle with. People want to feel like you are going to make their lives easier and to lift their agenda, not to use them as a conduit to achieve your own. People also want to work in their own self-interest. They do not want to contribute to things that go against their goals.

**4. They want to feel like the product is ‘sent from above’**

A personal mark of a good product is, ‘wow - this feels very intimate’. Sent from above is not a technical term, but it is my personal mark of a great product and what makes something truly stand out. Every experience someone has is creating a map in their brains. A good product traverses and notates on that map in a way that lets you know the creator has seen the map before and carefully considered it.

Think of what a good gift is: that is what a product should aim to be. One of my favorite things to do when I give gifts is to examine a person’s life and see what they have a lot of, or what they utilize a lot. A good gift to me is a better version of something that someone uses a lot, and that will be utilized often. A good gift shows that you pay attention to someone, have put thought into their life, and worked considerably to think about what would contribute to it without them having to do a lot of the leg work for it. In this way, a good social good product is like a good gift.

## Avoiding self-annihilating care.

A number of social service programs artificially deflate the amount of people they serve through strict criteria and friction-filled application processes. The mindset that often prevails is that if people really want help, they will sit through pain.

Although people want the core of what the product or service is offering, the amount of work they need to do to get it may be too much for them. It can feel humiliating, or is otherwise inaccessible and confusing. This is directly applicable to software. Oftentimes, software is easily able to act as a friction-reduction layer instead of a friction-addition layer.

Benefits navigation software shows what friction reduction is worth, and where it has limitations. GetCalFresh, a Code for America tool, rebuilt California's SNAP application into something you could finish on a phone in minutes.

It became the portal for over 70% of the state's online SNAP applications and served 6.2 million people, and over that same period, California's SNAP participation rate climbed from 66% to 81%.

When a friction-reduced path exists, people overwhelmingly take it, which is its own measure of what the original process was costing them.

Reminder programs, like texts nudging people to re-certify before their benefits lapse, have mildly mixed results. Some programs do report improved renewal rates, while randomized evaluations of similar nudges found modest-to-null effects.

The most feasible explanation is that a reminder helps when the remaining task is doable, and does nothing when the underlying process is still too hard. Reminders and simplification are complements, as opposed to substitutes. Reducing friction is partially about digitizing and making a process more accessible, but is moreso about resolving the underlying flow that feels burdensome. Digitally building an arduous process does not make it magically less arduous.

We can, if we do not look for it, continue to build on the idea that pain is a necessary part of accessing care. Incorrectly, we may assume people will stick through and hold on through if they really need the help at the other end. Half baked, or overwhelming features can creep into products. Interfaces that are not updated even when it’s known they cause confusion, or things that are left to be ambiguous because “users who really care will figure it out”, are subtle ways that we can encode pain, or take out our circumstances onto the people we want to help, in digital products.

This is also common in products where the operating entity or inventor has a high degree of burnout and strain, where half-baked or incorrect work items are shipped just to get them off the plate.

This is antithetical to the spirit of software for care. We know that general adversity for humans can build resilience, but friction often keeps people away from help and reduces the number of people positively impacted, more than any friction ‘helps’ users grow toughness that is valuable later.

Friction in specific cases may be of benefit, but in others may be a detriment. Inadvertently, lines of thought like this serve as a way to justify punishing users for being ungrateful or for needing care. Thoughts like these from people who work at an entity or product can be a sign of intense burnout or compassion fatigue, history of receiving or giving abuse, or lack of empathy or consideration. These are mindsets and circumstances that can eventually cause resentment towards the people creating products and the users, and reduce the effectiveness of a product.

## Courting unprecedented innovations.

Alternately, you may have your own unique idea of how to help and care for someone that is loosely based on previous versions of care, or has no clear analog. Like all other forms of software of care, the user must consent to it, and see the care as an incentive they want to receive.

New methodologies often face an uphill battle, especially relying on the laurels of your personal credibility. If you are a therapist with 40 years of experience in psychosis, your new method to help those with schizophrenia through an app may be taken seriously. If you are a mechanic with a schizophrenic brother, and you have come up an app idea, you will not have that benefit of the doubt.

**Products that are based on an entirely new innovation can often struggle to receive belief, funding, and face the necessity of establishing trust and validating their care methodology to an extreme level.**

One such example is a company called Mindstrong. They had 3 co-founders, one of which was the former director of the National Institute of Mental Health. Their new innovation was a smartphone app that would passively monitor interactions with the device and monitor mental health problems through changes in typing speed, phone usage, or other forms of communication.

At the time, Mindstrong’s method for surveilling mental health conditions through smartphone interactions was totally unprecedented. The company later ended up pivoting to a teletherapy model before shutting down in 2023. There was little to no evidence based research the company was able to produce that showed clear effectiveness of measuring interactions to mental health state. This lack of evidence later became an infamous hallmark of the company.

Mindstrong shows us how entirely new innovations can be a detriment. A paper was released in 2015 that further belies this paradox: Innovation can be negatively associated with startup survival rates, and a greater risk appetite can magnify these negative effects[^1]. One cited reason is that innovation adds complexity to the startup process, and creates a more fragmented environment for problem solving.

The best sorts of innovations are combinations of pre-existing solutions in a new light. For example, teletherapy combined the innovation of video calling software with psychotherapy, a form of medicine that does not require in-person visits to be helpful. Building an entirely unprecedented form of innovation without the resources and gumption to prove it can prove to be more dangerous to a startup, but can be required to create a moat or a truly new product on the market. Ideally, a social good product is combining a new innovation with a pre-existing technology or form of care to make adoption seamless when possible.

If you do wish to pursue a novel method of care, you should still follow a majority of the principles outlined, probably more than other solutions that previously have received public agreement of effectiveness.

It may even be a net positive for your solution to be unprecedented, as there are even some products that are held back by blanket acceptance of effectiveness towards the system they digitally adapted, such as mental health apps that repackage CBT methodology. An inventor trying to create an unprecedented care methodology should complete the same steps, perhaps with more sensitivity to negative data and with a more flexible grasp on what your system entails so that it can be flexibly modified.

## Creating ‘good’ care.

> Let's say that the person you love the most has just been shot. He or she is lying in the street, bleeding and screaming. A guy rushes up and says, "Step aside." He looks over your loved one's bullet wound and pulls out a pocket knife -- he's going to operate right there in the street.
>
> "OK, which one is the injured one?”.
>
> You ask, "Are you a doctor?”
>
> The guy says, “No."
>
> You say, "But you know what you're doing, right? You're an old Army medic, or …"
>
> At this point the guy becomes annoyed. He tells you that he is a nice guy, he is honest, he is always on time. He tells you that he is a great son to his mother and has a rich life full of fulfilling hobbies, and he boasts that he never uses foul language.
>
> Confused, you say, "How does any of that fucking matter when my [wife/husband/best friend/parent] is lying here bleeding! I need somebody who knows how to operate on bullet wounds! Can you do that or not?!?” Now the man becomes agitated -- why are you being shallow and selfish? Do you not care about any of his other good qualities? Didn't you just hear him say that he always remembers his girlfriend's birthday? In light of all of the good things he does, does it really matter if he knows how to perform surgery?
>
> In that panicked moment, you will take your bloody hands and shake him by the shoulders, screaming, "Yes, I'm saying that none of that other shit matters, because in this specific situation, I just need somebody who can stop the bleeding, you crazy fucking asshole.” "I don't get it. Would it help if I put on a lab jacket? Here, one sec, let me just …"
>
> So here is my terrible truth about the adult world: You are in that very situation every single day. Only you are the confused guy with the pocket knife. All of society is the bleeding gunshot victim.

Unfortunately, you are probably coming through to read this and may still be a bit lost on what your product should do, or how you come to feel what care is good enough. This is something I sympathize with often. Even at my jobs, almost every single place I worked, I always desired to do more for the user or to have more concrete proof that the good I was shipping was good enough.

The only way to truly determine is to interact with people and come to see how they view your idea. You simply do that, again and again until you feel as though it’s right, and that’s the next step.

There is an essay that was written in ‘Cracked’ in 2012 by David Wong, titled “6 Harsh Truths that will make you a better person”. It went viral for the time, and was picked up mostly by people who didn’t particularly understand what they needed to do in their lives.

There’s one analogy in the essay that I think about at least weekly, and that seems to get more poignant with time. It encapsulates directly what your relationship with your users often starts as, and sets a clear tone for what’s next.
