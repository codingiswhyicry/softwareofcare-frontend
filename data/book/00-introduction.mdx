I’m sitting on my best friend’s floor in 2017, watching as my Tumblr post about an app goes viral. It’s my app, Verena, an SMS-based security system for people living in dangerous situations. It’s supposed to text people’s loved ones when someone is in trouble, and to help them store the evidence they need to stay safe.

The post starts with 100 notes (the Tumblr version of likes and reposts combined), then 1,000. 12 or so hours later, and it reaches 25,000 notes and crashes the Google Form that people were asked to fill out. My Safari browser and 2015 MacBook Air stands frozen in fear, unable to render the webpage from all of the traffic to it. As a 15 year old, I reached out online to ask for a small favor, and what I was shown in return was more than anything I could have comprehended. I’m still looking for that comprehension today.

In 2017, I entered my security system application into a competition for young developers. Part of the competition was doing market validation research: In my spare time, I built the app and was ready to share it with the world. But, I had yet to complete market validation.

Now, I needed 10 or so people to say they would feel safer while having the app so I could apply. Out of 30,000 people that ended up completing the survey, 90% of respondents said they would feel safer with the application and saw immense value in it. I lost the competition, but I spun Verena and AnxietyHelper into my own non-profit, Astra Labs, which was supported by funding from TOMS and Teen Vogue in 2018.

I had been programming for roughly 4 years at the time, and had already released my first app to the public in 2015. By the time I got to creating my non-profit, I assumed I knew everything there was to know about helping people with software. “How hard could it possibly be?” I imagined, thinking I had unlocked the full scope of human knowledge, just like every 16 year old historically alive.

Perhaps back then, this book would be a numbered list with two items.

- build software.
- care.

Years deep into running my non-profit that was meant to help people in their darkest moments, I was sexually assaulted in 2021.

After that, I didn’t want to build software anymore. It was a perceived humiliation. Just 4 years earlier, I released an application for this exact thing. The one that crashed Google Forms, and my Tumblr account. My app Verena was meant to help people call others in their community and to store and address the details of the situation if they ever wanted to report it later.

I was unconsolable, and didn’t even believe it was assault for almost a year after. I don’t think anything in my life truly shook my confidence like that: Building an app for people who were in danger, people who were assaulted, and not even feeling like I had a reason to open it up.

Although I had an interface and held a system for care in my head, I didn’t want to “contact my community”. I didn’t want to “create an incident log for later reportation”. It felt like my own software mocked me, and my inaction and inability to cope or process what had happened. I believed if I tried to build an assault care application now, it would simply wire the user $500 and show rotating puppy gifs.

The disconnect struck me for years while building my next company in an entirely different space (wildfire). I felt inept at building software for others without a strong, passionate person pushing me forward. Thankfully, I found that in my co-founder at Faura.

But, the assault, combined with other circumstances made me entirely lose faith in my ability to ideate, find problems, and correctly solve them. If I built an assault app and yet still, was assaulted, what good was I at my job? How can I build my career around trying to solve the world’s hardest problems with technology, and yet be so blind to my own?

This disconnect that still lives on within has directly led me to write this book, and continue a lifelong search to find how to truly build software that meets people in their hardest moments. Not on paper, but in actuality.

In 2015, I released my first app when I was 13, Then ran a software development non-profit, Astra Labs, for 6 years, starting in 2018. We developed software specifically for people going through the hardest situations in their life. Astra oversaw AnxietyHelper, Verena, Everine, and created an open source library, Aureus, for non-profits working to build safety critical software. Astra also incubated Seon, an app for those with long term disabilities and their caretakers, and Emra, a website that auto-generated natural disaster response plans and triggered them when a disaster occurred.

On the side, I worked at the Multnomah County Office of Homelessness, where we managed applications for eviction relief programs in the Portland Oregon area. Medical device company, clinical trial software. Advisor for Headstream Mental Health Innovation, was an advisor for PBS’s Wellbeings series.

In 2023, I pivoted to a quieter and easier life. Trying to fight wildfires.

For fun, I’ve written a blog called ‘codingiswhyicry’ for the past 5 years or so about startups, non-profits, aviation accidents, technology, and how it comes to define humans day to day. Every essay and passing day, I learn more about the true nature of what I have embarked on, and how little previous software methodologies and advice has prepared me for this niche sector.

At the first time of writing, there was no clear concentration of this knowledge in an operational form made for the software industry. There were some guides for general social good innovation, textbook content that was not beginner friendly, or literature focused solely on non-profits, but I found them lacking in day to day practicality and applicability when coming to designing or developing software products. Especially in the startup industry, we were faced with almost conflicting information about how to build safe products, yet fast enough to get to market, at high enough growth rates to satisfy investors.

Constantly, I was reading about and seeing great companies and products who were getting it wrong and inadvertently hurting users (or not improving their life), and struggling to figure out how to do it “right”. This, ultimately, is the goal of the book. I want my search for substance on how to solve these problems to be distilled into an easier, and accessible form to give to you. Not an orientation on how we have gotten it wrong and how technology can ruin our future, but about how to develop it from the outset to reduce the likelihood of that happening.

I now feel that software that faces the public to provide social good is one of the most intellectually diverse and rigorous things someone can do in the tech industry today. This is for many reasons: building software that truly helps is a multi-disciplinary process, and it requires an intense amount of forethought, detail, commitment, and stress. You are often building software for the fringes: the fringes of users, resources, funding, implementations, and industry.

It also requires a great degree of philosophical and ethical considerations. What does it mean to care for others? How do you tend to users in crisis? How do you measure and prevent adverse outcomes, like an outage of a domestic violence reporting application? A lot of these scenarios, ones I’ve encountered before, require tiered and detailed approaches based on traditional engineering principles, combined with financial planning, nuance, and relationship skills that enable you to mold your idea towards building the product your users need.

The software industry is flush with cash. Seriously, so much cash. Global venture investors deployed $425 billion into more than 24,000 private companies in 2025 (up 30% from the year before) and then the first quarter of 2026 alone totaled close to 70% of all venture capital spending in 2025, an all-time record for a single quarter. More founders are creating companies than ever, and app stores and platforms alike are being flooded with new mobile apps, websites, and products.

At the same time, there is a growing disdain for tech companies and the impact they have on the lives of everyday people. Four years ago, 71% of Americans thought tech companies had a positive impact on the country; that number has plunged to 50%, while negative views climbed from 17% to 33%. Gallup now finds just 20% of Americans confident in major tech companies. This is a record low, down steadily from 32% in 2020. I, for one, agree with a lot of this disdain.

On the other side, tech for good has landed somewhat discordantly and disproportionately across the world. Some projects have been able to accelerate through to full market penetration and to survive at scale, and other deeply promising products have failed to graze the surface. Thus, the potential of software to cause harm is happening at full throttle, whereas its’ counterpart of good remains under-explored and underfunded.

This is the core motivation for the book: creating a form of shareable knowledge that can hopefully accelerate and concentrate what we do know.

This book is meant for founders, developers, or anyone else who wishes to build and be a good steward of this kind of product. This is a book I wish was written for me. I hope you come to see, as I do, that having the privilege and resources to build software in this way is one of the great responsibilities that has come to pass in our modern world, and I hope you join the challenge with me.

Developing software that cared for others saved my life. In my TED talk I gave, I discussed the development of my 2 apps that at the time, led me to start the non-profit Astra Labs. After struggling with severe mental health issues, multiple suicide attempts, being kicked out of schools leading me to drop out at 16, and receiving an Asperger’s diagnosis, Astra was the way it felt the most natural for me to connect with the world, and to give and receive it’s love.

Those apps gave me a connection to humanity and to my own life that I did not believe was once possible. I used to count all of the users for Astra as reasons why I shouldn’t take my life. Even today, it provides a through line to connect me to the world that I am forever grateful for. This work is my tether, and I would not wish to see the stranding that would occur if I was unable to indulge in it.

In my time, I have been a developer, designer, tester, engineering manager, product manager, CTO, and executive director. Being able to move through each part of the software development life cycle has informed this book, as it’s meant to be a guiding vision from conception, to design, to long-term company planning and management.

One of the core things that makes this so imperative is what I started discussing at the beginning of the chapter. Software, and the internet that carries it, have created unparalleled scale and flexibility. To be able to build products that serve people in their darkest moments and have 30,000 people echo the need for them was a small and early sample of that.

The vast reach and low barriers to making software are key traits of the harm it has come to bring. It also can enable it to tend to people through scaled intimacy in a grand way. This unique juxtaposition is what makes social good software development so unique, and compelling. It also is why a lot of standard advice needs to be re-written, or doesn’t fully apply.

Software is one of the most accessible, fastest, and cheapest ways to reach people. Technology developed in kindness is the greatest gift I have to offer with my life. I hope this book will help you to share it with others, as well.
