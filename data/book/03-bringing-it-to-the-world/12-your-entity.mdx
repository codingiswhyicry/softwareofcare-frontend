Everything in software is solvable when there is money. When you have no money, your ability to solve problems hits zero. Burnout is high and morale is dead on arrival. Everything about how much you can help people depends on how long you can last long enough to help them.

Generally, choosing the entity that will own your software product is choosing your flavor of shit sandwich.

Each will come with constraints and problems, and it’s best to choose the version that you as the inventor are able to manage the best, and that gives your software product the best and most fighting chance to survive. Every funding model is essentially a discussion of the tradeoffs that best work for your product, team, and goals that you have. Your funding model becomes what pushes you forward, and can be the life or death of the organization.

## Continuity is care.

Caring for users is something that can deepen and grow over time. The longer your software product is available, the more people can use it and can receive that care. Not every product should exist continually, as some may be time boxed or may solve the problem they were created for.

But for long-term issues, continuity of your software product will do a lot of good - the longer your product exists the more people use it, share it, and the more chances you get to develop and refine it into the best of its’ class.

Even more so, change and care for others takes an incredibly long time to see. Most change happens on the scale of years, or decades. Being able to release a product and see immediate wide-scale, sustained change is improbable. Therefore, being able to see how a product truly impacts people over the course of their life and what true outcome happens will take years.

## Economics of care.

The general unit economic cost of software aligns with most other unit economic models. The more of anything (API usage, hosting, etc) that you use, the more money you may save. Beyond that, the kind of cost per use of the software, and the actual impact and outcome you want it to have will determine the unit economics of the underlying software.

At Astra, because we built mobile apps with entirely volunteer based labor that bundled the back-end within the mobile-app binary, our cost per person helped was incredibly low (in cash): reaching $0.005. We were able to reach and help hundreds of thousands for little to no money, with no one on salary.

Ideally, the unit economics of the product will be known prior to releasing for development and creating an entity. The kind of entity you choose and the unit economics you have will define the struggles that you will face.

The unit costs of software are going to be uniquely good for a business, but perhaps uniquely bad for a non-profit organization. The unit costs depend specifically on your infrastructure, external vendors needed, technology within, and scale / compliance requirements of the product.

For example, any healthcare products that require HIPAA compliance are inherently going to have higher unit economic costs: most infrastructure providers require an enterprise contract or additional fee for HIPAA compliance. You will also potentially need to hire auditors, specialized contractors and knowledge workers, and have more complex system design needs for auditing and validation of compliance with the requirements.

## The models of entities.

### Venture-backed startup.

A venture backed startup is a company that has low unit economics, and can reach a massive market size. Most VC backed companies will need a way to get to a $1 billion valuation, and the primary objective of a venture capital company is to result in IPO or an exit from an investor perspective. The numerical laws of venture capital require a lot of investments in a wide variety of companies. A majority of those companies will fail, or not return a large amount of investment. However, a small percent will return the entire fund, and hopefully beyond. Venture capital companies are given a lot of money, with a lot of pressure to continually grow, find new markets, and evolve.

### Donation-funded nonprofit.

A non-profit software startup will often have one or more core projects, and will be funded through direct individual donations, foundation grants, or other gifted revenue.

As a non-profit, people will want to see lower overhead costs and higher program costs. It is worth figuring out ahead of time what and how you will label managing your piece of software to work within the contexts that donors want to see, while not stifling the unit economics that work to your advantage for scale. There are a lot of non-profits who indirectly have programs shaped by what donors want to see, not what users may actually find helpful.

Non-profits may also be subject to political will and be less willing to work on innovative projects, or anything that rocks the boat too severely.

### Bootstrapped for-profit.

A bootstrapped for-profit is a for-profit business that does not raise outside funding, or raises the minimal amount needed. It is known for being a uniquely difficult way to build a business, but one that often can provide more control and flexibility over the business and determination over its’ destiny in the long run. A bootstrapped company will often rely on the personal savings of one or more people, or the ability to generate revenue prior to leaving someone’s job full-time.

### Open Source Software Project

An open source software project is one where the code is available online, and able to be reviewed and contributed to by almost anyone within guidelines. This form of the software being “everyone’s” can make labor costs incredibly low, and can ensure longevity of the software by properly decentralizing key maintainers. However, if a key maintainer leaves or doesn’t grow the project outside of themselves and no one maintains it, open source software projects can often become ghost-towns, or backed up with requests.

An OSS project can be a non-profit, or it can have a for-profit arm.

### Hybrid non/for-profit.

A hybrid non/for-profit is a structure of companies that enables them to experience both the benefits of being a for-profit enterprise, and the benefits of a non-profit company. Often, the non-profit may own the for-profit underneath it. This structure works for research companies, non-profits who wish to earn revenue, or other entities who would benefit from this structure.

### Larger institutional project.

A project also can start within the context of a government initiative, or within a larger corporation who wishes to pursue a social good software project. These projects may have more dedicated funding but can be easily wiped away if key champions for the project leave the company. It will also be subject to more complex requirements, tech stack limitations, and the political contexts of that institution and the apparition of continuity many institutions need to maintain. As such, larger institutional projects tend to be slow growing and a smaller increment of innovation, yet higher scale form of impact.

## Attaining Funding

### Selling a product

The easiest way to make money is to directly sell your product, or a premium version of it to people who are willing to pay.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • Provides consistent source of funding.<br />• Revenue scales with the amount of people you serve<br />• No reporting requirements or funder related politics | • Can become a barrier for lower-income users<br />• Can put your care methodology in tension with pressure to convert paying customers<br />• Can require ethical consideration about what parts of the product should be paywalled | • Products that have a strong market demand.<br />• Products with recurring use where a subscription feels like a fair cost. |

### Selling a derivative of your product

A derivative of your product is selling the byproduct of it, either to a 3rd party or to an enterprise. A derivative of the product can be a machine learning model trained on your data, selling user data to data brokers, making an enterprise version of your product for institutions, or any other indirect way of selling your product.

This is often where the most ethically fraught revenue models can be.

Derivatives require a secondary level of consent: if you are building a derivative of your user’s data, do they know about it? Does it affect their life in a negative way? Is it able to be traced back to them? These are all considerations that you need to manage prior to building and selling a derivative product.

Data brokers, and reselling or sharing data to 3rd parties can easily get out of hand, or be abused - even if that abuse is not intended. Selling period tracker data can easily be used to construe if a user of the product becomes pregnant. Data brokers have been known to be bad stewards of sensitive data, and can become spots where stalkers, abusers, and governments go to source information without consideration from the law or due process.

B2B equivalents of your product are able to bypass a lot of these data-sale issues entirely, and can form a strong sales cycle. Consumer use can become the marketing tool and proof of impact for your enterprise product, and both can feed into product development motions.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • Provides a source of revenue that does not rely on directly monetizing users.<br />• Can subsidize free access for your users<br />• B2B versions | • Can be one of the more ethically fraught ways to monetize<br />• Aggregated or anonymized data can be re-identified<br />• A data derivative can make your data an asset in acquisitions or bankruptcies, leaving protections in the hands of someone else if legal protections are not properly set up. | • Products with enterprise variants that can be sold to institutions<br />• Products with a clean level of separation between what is being sold, and who is being served.<br />• Products that can be monetized without selling identifiable user data. |

### Government Grants

Government grants can be given to non, or for-profit entities. They often entail a lot of reporting requirements, and can be a lot of work to maintain and keep track of. They can also be more accessible to get if you are lacking in a network, and provide stable funding (depending on the administration). They are often unsuitable for newer products, or unproven products, and can create issues with the users you serve if the administration has been harmful to them previously or is antithetical to their goals.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • Democratized access to funding, less networking and rubbing shoulders.<br />• Larger sums than most foundations that are able to fund more long term work<br />• Non-dilutive, requiring no equity or repayment<br />• Government support can be a powerful credibility signal | • Reporting requirements and niche RFPs can make building a scalable software solution harder, and the applications and reporting can require heavy overhead and personnel.<br />• Requires often insider knowledge or the advice of someone who has written a government grant.<br />• Can be beholden to political agendas and shifting.<br />• May make some users who distrust the government skeptical<br />• Payment terms may require finishing projects or getting reimbursed, which can jeopardize cash flow. | • Products that are aligned with public programs, initiatives or mandates.<br />• Operating entities that can manage the application and reporting requirement burdens<br />• Later stage products that have strong proof of impact and evidence. |

### Foundations

Foundations are often private non-profits who disburse money towards projects that align with issues they want to fund. Foundations can put out private invitations or public calls to apply for grants, which mostly will fund non-profits, although some foundations provide small amounts of money to new for-profits.

Although grant funding can seem easy, many foundations do not invest long-term into the products they support. They often provide fixed-term grants focused on specific programs and issues, and can have onerous reporting and application requirements. The amount of money a grant from a foundation may bring can be a major bonus for capacity building of your software product. A good relationship with a foundation can serve your product well.

It can be especially hard to get foundation funding for newer initiatives or products, especially products that employ a unique or novel care methodology. The threshold for impact is incredibly high, and most foundations do not provide a large amount of money towards unproven products without good reason or prior experience.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • Can be entirely mission aligned money, and can develop clear allies in the field that can be leveraged in many ways.<br />• High return on investment depending on the grant size<br />• Helps to develop strategic partnerships with specific foundations.<br />• Grants can show proof of credibility for other funders<br />• No repayment or equity required. | • Restricted funds can limit the growth strategies that software products can follow. They can also shape programs that are fundable instead of programs that are what users actually need<br />• Overreliance on grants can cause singular dependency on foundations.<br />• Reporting requirements can add complexity to the workload and evidence gathering.<br />• Grants rarely fund the boring work, and prefer to focus on the program funding that is flashy.<br />• High evidence threshold, and strong proof of impact needed. | • Non-profits who have strong proof of impact,<br />• For-profits that match foundation criteria.<br />• Capacity-building needs, to scale something that already is proven to more people.<br />• Products whose impact can fit into a foundation’s focus or reporting category. |

### Individual Donors

Individual donors often give money in small amounts on a recurring basis, or a one-off basis. The premise is that enough individual donors need to be convinced enough of your product being a solution, in order to donate something. Donors often want to donate not because of what they’re giving to, but because what it says about them and enables them to be apart of. Managing donations as a software product comes with a lot of complications within the context of the regulatory space.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| • Great way to engage local community.<br />• Unrestricted money<br />• Recurring small donors can be an incredibly durable funding base that provides capacity for maintenance work<br />• Aligns accountability of the product to the public | • Can also have restrictions on it<br />• Requires built-out donation development function to ensure success, needs proof of impact.<br />• Amount given often can struggle to reach costs that initial software development may incur or that tech salaries are.<br />• Requires constant storytelling, and publicly showing the impact through user stories<br />• Donation can follow news cycles and media, not need of organization<br />• Managing compliance requirements of donors can require an immense amount of work | • Products that have emotional impact, and a broad swath of the public with the resources to support<br />• Entities willing to invest in growing community and communications as a core function of their work<br />• Products that have users who cannot pay, but who have stories that travel. |

### Venture Capital

Venture capital is a large sum of money for a small to medium percentage of ownership of the company. Venture capital can be hard to get, and can put companies on a “fundraising” treadmill. Many inventors also find that fundraising can become a full-time job and can sideline the process of building the product if they have limited resources.

| --- | --- | --- |
| • Largest sums of funding available for software companies<br />• Can often introduce additional benefits as a perk, like compute credits, introductions to those in their network, and potential partnerships with other portfolio companies.<br />• Signals credibility to hires and partners | • Not suitable for niche solutions, or unscalable solutions. ### Business Loans

A business loan is a fixed amount that your company will be expected to pay back. If you are just starting out, your chances of getting a business loan may be heavily tied to your personal financial situation.

### Accelerator programs

Accelerator programs are time limited programs that often provide money, networking, and education in return to “boost” or accelerate your company. There are many accelerators out there, and the ones that will be beneficial greatly depend on your company structure and value.

If you have previous business experience, you may not find an accelerator valuable for the educational content. You will mostly be looking to make sure you can optimize your network from it, or get a financial deal. There are also specific accelerators that act as a strong signal to other companies in the space, and being associated with one opens doors regardless of if you found the program beneficial.

| **Positives** | **Negatives** | **Suited for** |
| --- | --- | --- |

## Funding at the helm.

### Choosing the right funding.

The key rule of revenue is that it should be diversified to be sustainable. Many companies and software products that are successful have multiple streams of funding from different sources.

For-profits have funding through multiple forms of revenue earned through different features, tiers of products, or different products. This may look like a freemium model, developing both consumer and enterprise products, releasing a secondary API selling access datasets, or any combination of the form.

Non-profits have a combination of donations, foundation grants, earned revenue, long term gifts, and government grants.

The right kind of funding aligns with the same principles we’ve echoed in this book: a balance of sustainability and harm reduction to your end users. This is not an easy feat by any means, and ethically considering your users can place immense constraints on the business that feel short-sighted. Trust, respect, and consideration are muscles that you’ve needed to grow to build a good product for your user. It’s even more important that you utilize these practices when it comes to gaining and sustaining funding. No one wants to feel like they are the product sold, or that success comes at their detriment. This desire is even more amplified if you feel like a product is making money off of your pain, or suffering in a way you can’t see and without your knowledge.

### When the buyer is not the user.

Many software of care products with an enterprise offering can have a split down the middle when the person who uses the product is not the person who pays for it. A school district buys the reading app; the student uses it. An insurer buys the resilience assessment; the homeowner completes it. This is often the only long-term viable models for software of care, because the people who most need it can be the least able to pay for it. But it comes with a gravitational pull you need to understand and manage.

In our world, money is feedback, and all of it comes from the buyer. The buyer's complaints can reach and restructure your revenue streams, while the end user's feedback reach your inbox and nothing farther. When left unchecked over enough quarters, roadmaps drift toward whatever renews contracts and not on the end user’s experience.

The end user also can't fire you, nor even decline you, because the product arrives through their school, job, or benefits. A user who cannot leave and cannot pay gets exactly as much weight as you deliberately choose to give them. That is why it is important to ensure their weight and feedback is treated with gravity.

Sometimes the buyer's interests are not different, but actively adversarial. The employer funding the mental health app may want workforce stress data. The insurer funding the assessment may want grounds to non-renew the riskiest properties. At Faura, the tension we experienced was structural - insurers wanted complete risk data, and homeowners wanted lower premiums, but not cancelled policies. You cannot remove the tension. What you can do is decide what the buyer does not get before the contract is signed, and put it in the contract instead of the FAQ.

There’s many strategies to ensure the end user’s care and consideration through the process, but the lived reality of it will depend on what works best for you. Give the user real value on their side of the split, so their engagement is chosen rather than extracted. Keep users' outcome metrics first-class in your evidence harness even when no buyer asks. Often, that data is the only counterweight you will have in a roadmap argument.

You also, internally, will need to maintain an internal checks and balances system. If any stakeholder of a product finds it too out of balance for the, you will have an existential crisis on your hands. There are moments where you will often need to act as the mediator and to work in the middle between two opposing sides, and to learn how to be incredibly diplomatic. But, diplomacy is only effective while the parties see resolutions. If the buyer does not see the long term value, or the user feels like a data cow, the balance can be thrown off in a silent way that may spell the downfall of the product.

### Evading dependency risk.

This is particularly a problem for non-profit, or for-profit software companies that rely on extending access to government programs (like food stamps, disability, etc). This is primarily a focus in countries where funding of these programs can vary from state to state, or administration to administration.

If your software product’s core focus is acting as an additive layer on top of funding, you should strongly consider what your options are if that funding decreases, or ideally increases. Simply understanding that the product will cease to function is an option, as well as creating specific plans to direct users to other funding, or trying to lobby funding from a secondary source. Regardless of how you wish to handle it, what matters is knowing what major risks to your software projects are, and how you are going to get around them.

### Burnout as a funding problem.

Burnout seems to be an evergreen issue within the social good space, and one that is not going away any time soon. Not having enough funding, or not having enough funding consistency can force operating entities into survival mode. This is where the personnel is also expected to do a major amount of work for below-market rate, and to continually sacrifice themselves for the good of the mission. Not only does this lead to high attrition, it comes back to directly cut the amount of impact a social good product can have. When people see the amount of sacrifice that is required to get a job in the social impact sector, they often leave to more profitable jobs or jobs that enable a different kind of intellectual rigor.

Working in survival mode also reduces the time horizon someone is thinking on, in a way that eventually becomes detrimental to the organization. When you are just focused on solving immediate issues and managing crises, it becomes incredibly hard to make logical and evenhanded decisions about medium and long-term survival. This often means losing opportunities for growth, meeting strategic moments in the world and the market, and creating a self-cannibalizing culture of fear that doesn’t enable a stable environment to give consistent care.

Funding, above many things, is not only an investment into your product but into the functioning of your organization. If burnout or high attrition is not combatted, it can spiral thoroughly, leaving your impact and the care you wish to give to users as a minor consideration instead of a primary focus.
