As your product lives on in the world, two components of it determine how long it will last, and where it will go. Evaluation of your offering, and growth are two sides of the same coin. How effective is the product at providing a solution, and in what other ways can it provide it?

## Growth

Growth is an important and crucial context for social good software, and can be where the pains of the real world meet the pains of the choices you’ve made to get here. Focusing on it is something that comes with a healthy lifecycle of a product, but it brings its’ own form of mindset and mental load along with it.

Growth of your initial software product can come from different angles.

#1) the expansion of demographics that you’re targeting as users, from increasing age range to different countries, #2) The replicability of your care methodology into other circumstances, #3) the addition of new care methodology, #4) new features and products, or ways for the public to interact with your tool.

### Directing growth

Before embarking on the choice to grow, you should have a solid understanding of what the outcome will hopefully provide. Are you aiming to grow to raise funding? To scale your impact? Growth is something that should be undertaken with a direct purpose, and a direct measurement of success. When you choose growth, you also choose to add additional complexity and underlying mental strain.

Growth also, will not fix some issues. In fact, it may worsen them or reveal them more quickly than you can get them under control. Growth should be strategic to the larger goals of your entity, and not just growth for the sake of it.

### Tracing a baseline.

You should have a baseline understanding of how users are using your system and where they go. This baseline will shift, but essentially: come up with a group of metrics that give you at a glance where your product is. These should be coupled with the impact metrics. For example, number of releases, rollbacks, conversion rate to website, number of adverse incidents, etc. Anything that provides a general health check of where the product is and what is happening will be crucial down the line for quick check-ins and directional alignment.

A baseline depending on your operating entity can be a quick gut check by utilizing App Store Connect or Google Analytics, or it may be as in-depth of a BI analytics tool that sends updates to slack daily. What matters most is having consistent touch points with your product through growth that enable you to understand changes in user behavior and usage that may signal the need to make different investments or changes.

### Entering new horizons.

#### Growth into new problems

Growth into new problems means taking a proven care methodology and applying it to a different problem. The mechanism stays, but the circumstance or angle that someone is using the product for changes. A walkthrough that de-escalates panic attacks may also work for dissociation or cravings, or an assessment flow built for wildfire risk can also be extended for flood risk.

Whether the transfer works depends on whether the new problem shares the mechanism or just the surface. Panic attacks and cravings both respond to grounding and interruption, so the methodology carries over. Panic attacks and chronic depression look adjacent but operate differently, and a tool applied across both fails the second group while registering as growth. This is the quiet failure mode of this path, where a metric and TAM chart shows expansion while the new population receives care built for someone else.

Products that grow this way well tend to treat the new problem as a new product wearing familiar clothes. Ideally, taking the full process as though it is a new phrase of ideation is a great path. A product expansion through re-using the same mechanism can look deceptively easy, while not providing clear repeatability of impact. This means it’s more important to ensure that the evidence harness carries through and shows acceptable results for new circumstances.

#### Growth through product expansion

Growth through product expansion means the product itself grows through more offerings. In practice, this can look like new features, new front-ends, or other ways for users to interact with the same care. The problem itself and the target population stays the same, and the product wraps more of the circumstance around them.

This is the most natural-feeling growth path, because the requests often arrive on their own, or the product becomes more clearly defined and understandable as iterations continue.

Users ask for features, feedback backs them up, and each addition can seems small and an easy win against the totality of the product. The pattern to watch is accumulation that reduces the core product experience. Features that each made sense individually have the possibility to add up to a product that is harder to navigate for the user having the worst day of their life. Additionally, every new surface carries its own share of testing, moderation, failure modes, and maintenance.

These are costs that land and weigh on the team long after the feature ships. Feature bloat in commercial software annoys people, but in care software, a cluttered path to reducing the efficacy of the product because of how overwhelming it becomes.

Expansion tends to hold up when each addition maps back to the care methodology rather than to engagement. A journaling feature that feeds the user's own pattern recognition extends care; a streak counter borrowed from consumer apps extends usage. The distinction between outcomes and engagement from the measuring chapter does the sorting work here, and the ideal user session shows whether the product's center of gravity is drifting — whether the new features surround the critical path or bury it.

#### Growth through new products

Growth through new products means building something separate: a second product, under the same entity, usually for an adjacent problem or population. Where product expansion adds to one thing, this path multiplies the things.

It is the most expensive growth path, because almost nothing transfers automatically. Although previous learnings from the previous products will provide a strong base of institutional knowledge and operational abilities, a new product still means its own care methodology, its own duty of care, its own critical paths, evidence harness, moderation load, and maintenance. This a second full lifecycle running alongside the first, usually staffed by the same people. Usually, this kind of growth can strain a team further if they are already under stress, and don’t have the resources to accommodate for the greater desire for additional capacity.

What does transfer is organizational: the trust of the community, the research already done, the infrastructure, the entity, and the lessons paid for the first time around. But, entities tend to underestimate the split of attention and what it costs. The first product rarely gets cheaper to run just because a second one exists.

The path makes sense when the new problem genuinely can't live inside the existing product, like when the population differs enough that one interface can't hold both, or when mixing them harms one group. It also makes sense when paired with the capacity to take on this new product and to make it succeed, and when the distribution channels that already exist can be primed to contribute to the growth of a new product.

A tool for abuse survivors and a tool for people worried they are abusive may share research and even a mechanism, but not an app. When the boundary is real, a new product protects both sides of it. When the boundary is imagined, the result is two half-maintained products where one whole one could have lived.

#### Growth through partnerships.

Growth through partnerships means reaching new users or new capability through someone else's structure rather than building your own. The panic attack app gets embedded in a school district's counseling program, bundled into an employee assistance platform, or distributed through a crisis line's follow-up texts. The product stays roughly the same; someone else's channel carries it further than the organization could alone.

It is the cheapest path per user reached and the one with the least control over the experience. The partner's staff make the introduction, the partner's context frames the product, and the partner's incentives shape which users see it and when. A tool that people found on their own terms arrives instead through an authority — a school, an employer, an agency — and inherits whatever feelings the user has about that authority. The same product reads differently when the counselor's office recommends it, and for some users that endorsement is trust borrowed; for others it is surveillance implied.

Partnership growth tends to work when the partner's incentives are examined as carefully as the reach they offer, because distribution deals quietly recreate the buyer-and-user split: the partner becomes a second customer whose renewal matters, with all the gravitational pull that entails. The partnerships that hold up are the ones where what the partner wants from the arrangement is known, written down, and compatible with what the user needs from the product — and where losing the partner is survivable, since channels owned by someone else can close on their schedule, taking their users' access with them.

#### Growth into new demographics

Localization is the process of adapting your project towards a new geographic region or demographic. It often looks like adding additional translations to the application, adding more resources or items specific to a region targeted for expansion, or running or making a marketing campaign that focuses and is targeted towards this audience.

The most important thing when it comes to localization is being able to traverse cultural contexts and references. This is often where products can fail to expand into new markets, even if the underlying product design and features land overall well.

Users want to feel listened to, and understood prior to interacting with a product. Internationalization and localization requires being able to manage many different contexts that a user may be going through, and being able to make slight tweaks and nuanced changes that reinforce understanding a user’s mindset and potential objections. These cultural differences can seem minor in theory, but may cause an entire user segment or population to reject a product that would strongly benefit them.

For localization, it is the same general process as the initial user feedback you undertook to understand the problem, but more so focused in on a specific group or population. If you are looking to expand your domestic violence app within Europe, you would want to hire someone who is experienced in that market, or complete a majority of research within your team. Localization is best done by finding people who are locals or knowledgeable about the population and letting them guide the localization plan.

Especially if you do any content moderation within your software product (including scanning keywords locally to identify crises), you should strongly have someone from that region review your content moderation systems and triggers. They should also work with you to include local references that can have a crisis-related context. For example, saying you are going postal in England may not be a problem, but saying it in relation to your workplace in America could require a secondary consideration.

Depending on your learnings, you may come away with one or more tasks to do to expand.

Localization or internationalization often bring:

- Translations of different languages to a piece of software
- Marketing materials tailored to a specific region or segment
- Content within the application that changes based on region or previous selections
- Expansion of previous categories to include applicable categories to your new users
- Expansion of content moderation or crisis escalation systems
- Testing pre-existing user input forms to make sure different forms of information are accepted

### Decisions of growth

#### The interplay of scale and growth

Often, you may be pushed to scale to different markets or towards different issues. This will mostly be an issue with VC backed companies, where broader push to scale can lead to the development of less-niche software, which then is less impactful for the users. You may need to make the tradeoff of making the product more generalized or complex for more growth, at the cost of increasing overhead to work on your software product and potentially diluting its’ impact. Sometimes you may get more resources to make the product more broad, but occasionally you won’t.

Vulnerable users also may feel like you are leaving them behind, or will be spending less time focused on their needs as a whole. The decision you should make is dependent on the goals of your operating entity. But, you should understand that there is always tradeoffs when it comes to scale and impact. Whatever you decide to focus on will inherently take away focus from preexisting initiatives. It is up to you to accept, or mitigate that ahead of time.

#### Trimming the hedges while the house is on fire.

Growth is a formidable mechanism for expanding impact. However, it can be as titled, trimming the hedges while the house is on fire. Many entities go on the path for growth with broken unit economics, a lack of understanding about their problem, or a product that just plainly does not work for the problem they are trying to solve. Growth brings benefits, but also more problems than it originally solves in some instances.

## Evaluation

Evaluation is the progress of figuring out how well a product or program is functioning in the world. This is different from impact, which quantifies the numerical amount of effect a product has. Evaluation determines the quality, and repeatability of your product.

### Creating an evaluation framework.

An evaluation framework starts with your theory of care written as a chain you can check: we do X, which causes Y for the user, which leads to Z in their life. Evaluation walks that chain link by link and checks where it holds. The impact work from earlier in this chapter tells you *whether* the endpoint happened; evaluation tells you *why*, *for whom*, and *whether it would happen again* — which is what repeatability means. A product that helped forty people is a good deed. A product that can say which parts did the helping, and for which users, and under what conditions, is a program.

For each ‘link’ in the chain, name what evidence would show it's working, what evidence would show it isn't, when you'll collect each, and who sees the results. Ideally, it should properly shape around the pre-existing evidence harness, using the evidence outlined to test against pre-established outcomes and to make a clear judgement.

Upfront, determine whether a given round of evaluation is formative (feeding the next iteration, allowed to be scrappy) or summative (judging whether the thing works, needs rigor). Mixing the two can produce evaluations that are too heavy to iterate with and too loose to prove anything.

### Choosing an evaluation method.

An evaluation method should match to the stakes and the stage, as opposed to prestige. Early products with small user bases should have methods that surface the *why*: interviews, observed sessions, support tickets read as data.

Maturing products that are competing for funding or searching to determine outcomes over years need methods that establish *whether, like* pre/post measurement, comparison groups, and eventually controlled designs. The failure mode in both directions is mismatch of methodology to maturity of the prepuce. Ideally, you would not running a heavyweight trial on a product still changing weekly.

Determining your method of evaluation should evolve around 3 considerations:

- What decision will this evaluation actually inform?
- How wrong can we afford to be?
- What can we ethically and financially collect?

### Randomized Control Trials.

The gold standard for evaluating a product, or program is an randomized control trial, (or RCT).

In an RCT, participants are randomly assigned to receive the product or not, and the two groups are compared. Randomization is the entire trick: it makes the groups alike in everything except your product, so a difference in outcomes is attributable to you rather than to the fact that people who download panic-attack apps differ from people who don’t.

An RCT’s high weight comes with a high cost. It can take time (often a year or more), money, sample sizes small products don't have, usually an IRB or ethics review, and a product frozen stable enough to be a consistent treatment.

Waitlist designs, where the control group receives the product after the measurement window, soften this and are the standard answer for care contexts; withholding a crisis intervention from people in crisis is not a design you should accept for cleaner data.

If a full RCT is out of reach, the family of quasi-experimental designs — pre/post with a comparison group, staggered rollouts measured as natural experiments — buys real credibility at a fraction of the cost, and funders who know evaluation respect an honest quasi-experiment more than a bad trial. Partnering with an academic lab can also put an RCT within reach: they bring the methodology and the IRB, you bring the product and the population, and the publication motivates them as much as your evidence needs do.

### Surveys.

Surveys are a multi-tool of evaluation that work for a variety of circumstances and products. They are cheap, scalable, and easy to do badly.

Work to find if a validated instrument exists for what you're measuring, use it instead of writing your own questions — scales like the PHQ-9 for depression or GAD-7 for anxiety have known reliability, clinical thresholds, and comparability to published research, which turns your survey data into evidence instead of vibes.

Second, respect and build around the response burden. Long surveys select for your least busy, least distressed users, which can quietly biases every result toward "it's working”. Short in-product pulses at natural moments beat quarterly questionnaires nobody finishes.

In addition, make sure to look for patterns in who *didn't* answer. Non-response is still useful data, and can be used to find dark corners where real insights about actualized impact lie. A user who churned in crisis does not fill out exit surveys, and if you only evaluate the people still around to praise you, you have built a machine for confirming yourself. Tie survey timing to the ideal-session baseline so you're measuring the same moments across users, and route the results into the harness with everything else.

### Decisions of evaluation

#### Triaging feedback.

Feedback will be your ear to the ground, and the ‘vitals’ of your product. You will always want a steady stream of feedback, and to come up with a way to close the feedback loop so that users feel that they are getting a valuable and heartfelt thank you for the time invested into your product.

Only work to gather the feedback that you are actually going to use, otherwise users may feel their trust is broken.

Make sure to triage feedback from multiple places, especially unsaid feedback. There’s a lot of tools that can be embedded into interfaces that can watch as users walk through the flow, can detect rage-clicks and points of confusion.

#### Affectations of users.

Remember in previous chapters that we discussed users may have a range of pre-existing emotional states from the experiences they have had. Depending on the users you serve, they may be especially overtly complimentary or negative. When you examine user feedback, it’s important to be able to cut through the emotional variances to determine the truth of what’s happening. Be ready to engage with users’ potentially emotional feedback with more direct, and objective questions.

For example, take a tool to calm down that is being used by a manic user who has bipolar disorder, and another that is experiencing a flashback from PTSD. The manic user may rate the tool incredibly high due to the euphoria they may experience, whereas the user with PTSD may not find the tool effective and lash out negatively in a review. There is truth in both of those pieces of feedback, but it’s important to objectively measure over time and across the cycles of emotion a user may experience to get the best understanding of impact, not just at one time.

Feedback that is collected should be varied from multiple sources, and the more variable the user feedback will be, the more it’s important to gather feedback from secondary experts or stakeholders as possible. This doesn’t mean that you should discount user feedback, as it can be too easily to wave away problems with the underlying care methodology or product as users being prissy or annoying. User feedback should be balanced with other methods that allow you to correctly contextualize and calibrate it.

It’s also worth mentioning that you may get hateful and purposefully cruel feedback. In this, deleting it from public view, ignoring it, or publicly denouncing it so other users see that you don’t find the feedback acceptable is the best thing you should do. With hateful public feedback, you should focus on making sure it does not hurt or taint the experience for users who will actually benefit from your product. On the other hand, it can get very mentally challenging to be doing content moderation across platforms, and letting it be while reporting the most grievous examples is another solid option.
