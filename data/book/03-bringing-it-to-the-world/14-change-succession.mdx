For over 20 years, the National Eating Disorder Association (NEDA) ran a hotline for years that they used to support those in eating disorder recovery.

They shut down the hotline in 2022, and then introduced Tessa in 2023 - an AI chatbot who started offering diet advice to recovering users with a history of ED. One such user was an eating disorder activist who posted what she found on Instagram, saying that if she had conversed with such a chatbot, she would have never recovered from her eating disorder and that Tessa’s advice could have even contributed to her death.

While only 2 people publicly reported receiving negative advice, Tessa was immediately pulled offline. To this day, it’s an example of how AI is not ready to intervene in critical mental health settings. It’s currently undetermined why exactly Tessa was introduced: NPR suggested that Tessa was created to replace 6 staff and 200 volunteers who were attempting to unionize the hotline, while NEDA president denies that and says the product was meant to reduce long-wait times and other pains of scale.

NEDA, in part made this change because of the COVID pandemic and changes to the mandated reporter structure that changed how their hotline could behave. NPR’s reporting broke the story in full, stating:

> “NEDA's leadership denies the helpline decision had anything to do with the unionization, but told NPR it became necessary after the COVID-19 pandemic, when eating disorders surged and the number of calls, texts and messages to the helpline more than doubled. Many of those reaching out were suicidal, dealing with abuse, or experiencing some kind of medical emergency. NEDA's leadership contends the helpline wasn't designed to handle those types of situations.
>
> The increase in crisis-level calls also raises NEDA's legal liability, managers explained in an email sent March 31 to current and former volunteers, informing them the helpline was ending and that NEDA would "begin to pivot to the expanded use of AI-assisted technology."
>
> "What has really changed in the landscape are the federal and state requirements for mandated reporting for mental and physical health issues (self-harm, suicidality, child abuse)," according to the email, which NPR obtained. "NEDA is now considered a mandated reporter and that hits our risk profile---changing our training and daily work processes and driving up our insurance premiums. We are not a crisis line; we are a referral center and information provider."”

Managing change as a product grows and evolves in the world is fraught, and can feel spiked with high levels of consequence and fear. With change comes succession: of a product, vision, staff, or even of a company that once seemed primed to innovate in the field it is currently considering leaving. Managing change is essential, and will happen to any product with longevity. Navigating it is in a sense, an accomplishment itself. But succession is how you stick the landing, and leave a lasting impression of the product: even if it means shutting down the product itself.

## Addressing change

Care that you give users must change as the circumstances surrounding them changes and continues. This is for a multitude of reasons. The circumstances facing the user will evolve, as well as the research and funding within the field. It should be of mind to always be keeping tabs on what is happening within the community you serve, as well as other initiatives coming to the market and major funding changes or research breakthroughs that may trickle down.

Software of care is a living thing, addressing living things and aiming to provide care for them. In preparing for change and knowing when it is going to happen, you put yourself in a better position to be advantageous to leveraging it.

### Tracking the pace of change.

Change within your product and its’ greater context should be tracked based on the usage metrics, news, what you hear from your own personal network, from your partners, and from what you see moving in the space. Staying abroad of what is continually happening is a crucial part of being able to contextualize your product, and to know how effective your solution still is. There also is a lot of attrition of information, especially in fields that are going through constant evolutions and research. What seems like common knowledge can often be overturned, or shifted in a significant way with small nuances that grow into common knowledge over time.

Personally, I set aside time daily to review what is happening in the software of care space. I set Google Alerts for relevant terms, where news is delivered weekly. I use social media to follow people I trust in the space. I also regularly review data outside of the news and social media, and check charts directly myself. Reviewing App Store and market place charts, using tools that estimate usage, tracking Google searches over time, and more help place a pulse on what is happening in your field outside of community and press coverage.

It can also be a good idea to read press releases from funders, like foundations, private equity companies, or venture capital firms. This can surface new companies before the greater industry is aware of them. There are also certain companies that I have enjoyed watching grow over time. I personally keep a list of software for care initiatives that I check in on perhaps once a year.

This is especially good practice if you have partnerships with these companies. Keeping track of product announcements or features is a good opportunity to continually pitch and grow the relationship, or to recognize strategic drift that grows with time. Even more, being able to know your history will serve you deeply in terms of building partnerships and knowing where the waves are.

### Contending with change.

As you develop your product in the market, it will become clear what components of change are easier to undertake than others. There are some features and offerings of the software products that will be ‘statues’ and others that will be more flexible, or lose cultural power over time.

Knowing what is a candidate for change is why it is important to know the status of what is being released in the market. There have been a non-zero number of times that I have worked on a feature just to see it get scooped up by a bigger company with a way bigger market share. Then, our usage declines and it makes more sense to focus on alternate features.

However, make sure change is grounded in user behavior, and not industry sentiment entirely. Industry sentiment can be marketing and fear, just as much as truth. There may also be cases where user behavior is not yet showing a change, but you have reason to believe or a hunch that something else may be a better proposition. This is where having a solid relationship with your users, and especially building a testing base will serve you well.

You can simulate and estimate user behavior to new features without actually jeopardizing the reputation and trust they have in you. This is also where being able to track adverse harm events will serve you well. Keep new changes, especially safety critical ones behind feature flags, track user behavior and adverse harm events, and let user behavior and your gut ultimately make the final decision on change.

### Being taken by change.

Unfortunately, there will be times where the market has shifted so much that it may not even recognize the need for your product. This has happened to me only in one case, and the water had changed so much that I felt we moved too late.

AnxietyHelper was the first app I built, and I released it in 2015. This was right on the cusp of a massive cultural conversation around mental health, sadly spurred by many public suicides and mass shootings. This was also combined with a push from Democrats to form a resistance to Trump and to empower youth activists, all waves that I was able to push the product forth on.

However, in 3 years - it became apparent that the product that I built as a broke teenager did not last compared to the amount of VC funding coming into the space.

Moreso, I did not particularly have a moat or a clear reason why users came to AnxietyHelper, besides the tools. I realized the broad market of mental health was going to sweep us away and that we would better serve our users (the majority of who suffered stronger mental health disorders), and would focus on more niche tools for users struggling with more acute mental health issues. We were able to re-affirm our commitment to our users, and to build a more niche market for ourselves where we could be more competitive and effective.

There will be times when you have to self-cannibalize. When something lands that completely undoes your model, and that sitting where you are is sitting to die. This is a terrible place to be if you are not prepared, and a great place to be if you are. Once again, user behavior, market share, charting, usage metrics, and discussions from your users are what you should optimize for. Following or hearing industry trends too much on things like this will make you vulnerable to being spooked, perhaps before there is a reason. Industry trends, and what CEOs say publicly can often be show-boating.

I always believe that anything a company says is a bit of propaganda. Make sure that if you are taking information seriously that changes the direction of your software product that you thoroughly think through what implications and benefits the person sharing that information will gain from you believing it.

It is also worth noting that self-cannibalizing a project is a very serious undertaking, and one that may completely break your users’ trust in you. It also may signal serious financial difficulties, which can spook teams, users, and partners. It is important, especially the longer the product has been on the market and the more people who rely on it, that the transition plan of decommissioning a product is thoroughly thought out and debated, and needs to propose a strong and convincing future. We will discuss sunsetting a product further down.

## Succession

Unfortunately, starting and developing a software product and bringing it to market is the easier part of the equation. As Logan Roy knew well, keeping a product in the world long enough to make a strong impact throughout its life is the second, and harder part.

Succession is the act of planning for continuity of your product, company, personnel, or components of your work that you want to outlast you. Its’ often best planned ahead of time, and continually re-evaluated as you go. Preplanning is paramount, because when you need a succession plan is when you least often want to have one.

### Continuing knowledge progression.

If you are often working with other people, you may constantly have knowledge gaps and perhaps consistent issues with ideas falling out of minds faster than you can implement or organize them. This is exceptionally an issue with volunteer-based organizations, fast-scaling startups, or OSS (Open Source Software) projects where information is concentrated by a select few engineers or team members. This is a very hard problem to solve - you must build the tradeoffs between documenting and training newer engineers and other personnel, with the tradeoff of losing work capacity initially to prolong it later.

This is especially an issue with software of care, where special attention needs to be paid architecturally, or the testing and prototyping side. The specifics of how you can handle this will depend on the entity structure. This will especially be an issue if there are special development processes that you have, such as specific safety critical testing needs or accessibility testing needs.

A lot of this can also be managed through process, like:

- Incentivizing developers to document their work
- Not merging PRs unless relevant documentation or testing work is done,
- Making sure that you spend a good amount of time with newer developers to enable understanding about how to test for accessibility
- Continually demonstrating the behavior that you want to see
- Working to go through shared knowledge systems and tag people in for updating information
- Setting aside dedicated time on a consistent basis to re-evaluate shared documents and update them.

All of these things are important, but require a top-down push for documentation and understanding the reasons why such processes exist.

Informally, I have while at CTO of Faura, made a specific personal system for myself that tracks notes about each company, notes about products and how my thoughts have shifted over time, 1x1 notes, and any other information that people would need to understand my job and take over as me. These notes serve the purpose of: 1) making me organize and synthesize the information in my brain, but also 2) making jumping off points for others not from scratch.

The most important thing about knowledge gaps is not starting from scratch entirely. If a volunteer with specific knowledge about accessibility technology leaves without having written anything down, it will be immensely more stressful than asking someone to update documents that they have already written and are in the habit of maintaining. Reducing knowledge gaps is about making sharing knowledge an incentivized habit, and not making it feel as a task that someone should only do prior to leaving the organization.

It also works to reduce burnout to make sure knowledge is shared, and increases the safety of deployments in your organization. If only one person knows your infrastructure or how to fix your CI/CD pipeline, it will be very stressful if that person is out while you are addressing a bug that affects sending messages about medical care. Building for redundancy of information is choosing safety when you are working with vulnerable communities.

### Ensuring Business Continuity.

Especially if you have an enterprise offering that you sell to business customers, one of the first things they will ask you is about business continuity. If something goes wrong, like a natural disaster, outage, or major disruption, how are you responding to make sure it doesn’t result in a problem for your clients? Regardless of if you serve enterprise customers, I would encourage you to provide a continuity plan for your software of care. By caring for people and offering it as a publicly available product, I think you have a bit of a duty to make sure you pre-plan and enable that care to continue.

You can model this plan after most business continuity plans, but the primary thing is making sure it’s a plan that works for your product, and will actually be used when needed. The most important thing when it comes to building any process is to make sure it is usable, and not over or under-engineered. If it’s a checklist on your MacBook, so be it.

This plan should take into account your team, and your software product. For example, if you are a solo founder and you have a server at your house, perhaps you identify a loss of power as a problem that can affect your users. As such, it might be in your continuity plan to get a cheap backup power supply that you make sure is enabled prior to a storm or in the first 5 minutes of a power outage.

The plan should vary in scope and scale depending on your team, and what components that your software product may have. This should be separate from an incident response plan, where an incident response plan focuses on software, security, or infrastructure related problems, business continuity will focus on continuing operations that are interrupted by physical outside world circumstances.

### Allaying succession.

Succession for software of care is one of the best things you can do to ensure its legacy. It also will be changing and different, depending on your organization and software product. Succession can mean figuring out and training successive engineers, executives, or people to take your role after you are gone. It can also mean finding a secondary home for your software product, if you are unable to build a sustainable organization that can financially operate it. As mentioned in the last chapter, succession may even be building a new product, or decommissioning an unsuccessful product.

Succession can also mean finding a home for your software product by turning it into a piece of open source software, writing essays about it and making clear your knowledge and learnings, attempting to sell the IP to a bigger or different institution as an acquisition or acquihire, or just finding a different project to give your passion to.

It is statistically the case that your software product may fail to find a long-term footing in the market, and it would be a wise idea to think about what you would want to extend survival to. Is it the engineering technique that you pioneered? A feature for people to more easily access hotlines? **Find what is the most impactful to you about the project and plan for how you want to enable it to continue.**

It is not a failure to build a product that does not last eternally. As an inventor, progress is not about building one software product that lasts for eons, but about building a collection of products over time that sustains upon the teachings you’ve received. There was, prior to shutting down my non-profit, a lot of grief and guilt I carried. I also delayed shutting it down quite longer than I should have to give myself hope, but at the cost of deluding myself that my personal interests were in the interests of the company.

Succession and being able to imminently prepare for it is a major pressure relief valve, especially because software and social good can be incredibly intense and unrelenting fields that lead people to burn out. Having options, and being able to see what your exit may look like before you are forced into it, can give you and your software product a “pressure relief” valve that will hopefully reduce your burnout and provide an alternate way for you to ensure safe passage of what you truly wanted to give to the world.

### Terminating a product.

Sunsetting a product is the ongoing process of removing it from the market. It is, for that product, its’ final arc within the users life and the last impression they will have of that product. How exactly you will sunset the product depends on the exact way it is built into the lives of your users, and the resources your entity has. How much detail and leeway you have in sunsetting should be correlated to the amount of active users and data on your product. The pillar I mentioned from the first chapter, that specifies that care meets its obligation from beginning to end, is either honored here or revealed to be decorative.

You should calibrate your notice period to user vulnerability as opposed to convention. Thirty days is fine for a budgeting tool. A panic attack app, or anything woven into someone's crisis response, needs enough runway for users to find, install, and grow comfortable with a replacement before yours goes dark - sometimes on the timescale of months instead of weeks. Your timescale should be enough to get the majority of users notified, resettled and to properly accommodate for the internal changes that will also inevitably come with sunsetting a product.

Forming a communication plan that spans all channels, clearly communicates an end date, and communicating multiple times is a good first step that encompasses most of what you will need. Then, decommissioning the product and starting to remove the infrastructure below it, closing vendor accounts, and doing any other work to preserve or store the leftover data or to delete it is a good plan.

It’s ideal to announce in the product itself, not just by email. Your most vulnerable users can be often the least reachable ones. Although it may feel embarrassing, it will be worse to under-announce than to over announce and have someone come looking for help they cannot receive.

It’s also a smart idea to hand off warmly, when you can. Naming and linking specific alternatives is the bare minimum. Be honest about the gaps between what you offered and what they do. If nothing replaces you, say so, and say what partial substitutes exist. Build your communications off of what works best for your users and how to transfer them to an equivalent, and not entirely as a eulogy for your product.

Data management should also be a consideration that’s pre-determined. Ideally, you should let users take their data with them and make export available from the first announcement, in formats a human or a successor product can actually use. Then delete what user data remains. Deletion is one of the last protective acts you can perform for people who trusted you.
