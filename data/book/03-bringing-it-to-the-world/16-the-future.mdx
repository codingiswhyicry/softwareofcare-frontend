  For nothing is fixed,
  forever, forever, forever,
  it is not fixed;
  the earth is always shifting,
  the light is always changing,
  the sea does not cease to grind down rock.
  Generations do not cease to be born,
  and we are responsible to them
  because we are the only witnesses they have.
  The sea rises, the light fails,
  lovers cling to each other,

I remember the day I knew my company was going to change forever.

It was December 2025 in Williamsburg, New York. I was looking out of the window at an office on the phone with my cofounder, with me holding back tears in front of strangers. We had been in business for almost 3 years, and it was apparent that we were struggling to land a product that resonated in the market. However, we still had another 1.5+ years of runway. We were about to launch a new product, and had lots of interested customers. Other insurtech startups would stop us at conferences or find us on LinkedIn and beg to understand our brand, or how we were getting in the door with the carriers that we were.

That didn’t matter. We were starting to make choices out of fear. We were no longer brave young warriors charging into the night, but transfigured into flesh balls of anxiety and stress as we burned millions and worked 17 hour days to see growth of our solution.

I watched my cofounder’s face through the screen, as she looks into the abyss intently. Then I carefully said something to her along the lines of, “The team can probably tell we’re freaking out. Another pivot makes us look weak in the market and dilutes our messaging. If we don’t have confidence in what we do, then no one else will. We need to lead from faith in what we bring from one product and one customer, instead of fear that it will not be good enough by targeting multiple products to multiple customers”.

We got off that call, and left for Christmas.

In January, when we came back from holiday break, our board told us (cordially) we were a failure and should shut down our company. It wasn’t even a year since we announced our $3.5 million seed round. I know we never did truly cross the threshold from fear to faith. I think about that conversation often, and wonder if I was talking to myself more than I was talking to her.

Unfortunately, right now feels like a very similar time in the “tech for good” space. Although it was very publicly lauded in the mid and late 2010s, tech for good has notably failed to contribute to major structural changes promised. In fact, even worse. AI tools, which are packaged as a productivity tool for the general populace, are threatening to eat jobs and contribute to greater economic inequality. Not only has tech for good failed to promise the progress it was desired to have, tech products and interacting with them may now actively cause more harm to users.

Investors, having tightened their belts post-COVID, are also incredibly cognizant of the lack of ROI on tech for good, and have pivoted to wellness as an adjacent category with more revenue and less direct constraints. Wellness can be defined (to me, at least) as tech for good for people who can afford an OURA ring or a Prenovo body scan.

Non-profits have also faced a funding crisis, in part because of the pullback of consumer spending leading to decreased donation, increase in the need to replace social services cuts, but also due to massive cuts from the Trump administration to funding - both of public and private programs. There’s not even program funding available for the organizations on the ground doing life changing work, like processing rape kits or providing mental health services.

The U.S non-profit sector is a bloodbath of federal funding cuts, lack of corporate donations, and low levels of donor support. Astra Labs shut down in 2024. Callisto, an organization that I loved and saw as an aspirational big-sister to Astra, built software that detected serial sexual predators on college campuses. They shut down during the writing of this book due to lack of funding.

How is this possible? A serial predator detection company is being left adrift without funding in a decade where the biggest news stories and political angling is regarding these predators. We have the attention to watch their destruction permeate in communities for years after, but not the funding to find and prevent them before they cause the majority of their damage.

On the other hand, for-profit software is no longer the underdog of the 70’s, 80-90’s. It is now one of the wealthiest corporate categories of the United States. Software, because of the unit economics and lack of physical assets that comes with it, is considered to be one of the most profitable, highest margin businesses we can build.

Rarely, does the software we let into our lives deliver on its promise without breaking us individually and societally in a myriad of ways. This book is an attempt to show there can be an alternate path to that. There is a world, where perhaps software can deserve the level of optimism the general public once had for it. Software can be more than antithetical to human progress and care, but as a scalable co-conspirator towards it.

We must try, with all of the resources flowing into the field, to do more than just what the field has given us today. I do not accept the current state of the consumer software field as the best we can offer, nor do I want to. It would be a sad day to think the best thing we can program and give back to the world is AI-nudify apps, FaceTune, generative AI chatbots, and Uber Eats. Is this the digital legacy we want to leave in the hands of the public?

I know, despite the pain, that I would much rather build something that truly and deeply reaches people - even if it fails or never makes it to the full vision we have for it. Consumer software is a reflection of us. Software is one of the most human things we have: often hurried, ambitious, naive, worn, and finding its way into a world that we have never had before. If we don’t like what we see, then we must start constructing a different face.

We have brought programming to the masses, and domesticated glass to do our bidding. Can we deserve the alchemy that we’ve found in our hands? Will we find a way to build it to enrich others? I don’t know. I don’t think it matters as long as we try.

What I wish to impart is that the future of care is going to be what we face it as. If we determine it is a lost cause, that all is failed and that technology is in the hands of powers that fully prevent software of care, then it is done. If we have faith that we can build something tangibly beneficial, even just the smallest win, we will have a chance of it.

If we decide it is not worth the vulnerability of faith, possibility leaves the room and apathy sits in its’ absence.

We will define, and have the choice to define, the future of what software of care looks like. It will not be easy. It should not be easy. We are aiming to build software that people can come to in the hardest parts of their life. We are designing databases that may hold information about child abuse, or sexual assault. We are defining interfaces that people may see as they navigate help for wanting to take their life.

This is an immense responsibility, and there is no way around it. But we must have confidence in what we bring to users, before they grow confidence in it themselves. We get that confidence from the work that we take to know the foundation is strong: from the evidence gathering, risk assessments, design process, and constant evolution of what we will build.

Confidence comes from action. There may never be a time when we feel like what we will build is enough, or that we can put the worlds in our head. But we don’t have to.

Users don’t need us to be perfect. They don’t need the most pixel-perfect screens, or iconoclastic branding. They do need our effort, our consideration. Software of care is just that: our care for our users encoded, so that they are ultimately able to access better outcomes for their life. We need to have confidence in our effort, and in our care. With effort and consistency, the future of care is unending.

I do not know if I will ever start a company any time soon, or if I will achieve the standards I wrote in this book. However, I do believe that this spark, whatever it is, can grow perhaps much brighter in others than it would remain alone in me.

I know that the path I have given you seems cumbersome. It’s meant to be. That’s why building software of care is so hard, and why getting it right means so much. Caring itself is hard. That is why it is so healing when we can get it right, and why it means so much to others when delivered correctly. Translating that into a digital artifact or an institution, is much more so. Constraints given to us by the world and the systems we build to meet them are at the heart of what makes engineering a dark magic.

Software of care will not come from ‘Request For Proposals’ from funders. It will not come from conference sessions or startup pitch competitions. It is summoned by us, but only if we want it and believe it can happen, despite the evidence that shows us how hard it can be. It will come from us, and our commitment to engraving it into what we delve towards. Care for others is a survival mechanism just as much as it is a form of love. Let us use the great resources around us to enable more survival, more care, and more love.

Software of care comes from you and I. In us, I have hope.
