I read a lot of non-fiction, and there are not many moments where a single chapter embeds and twists into my brain.

*No More Tears*, Gardiner Harris's investigation of Johnson & Johnson, was one.

EPO is a hormone that tells bone marrow to make red blood cells. Amgen synthesized it, and it was first approved in 1989 for dialysis patients, whose treatment destroys red blood cells. This was a great market for the product, and met a true patient need.

Johnson & Johnson licensed the drug and sold it as Procrit, and in the 1990s it was extended to chemotherapy patients as an all-purpose lift: less anemia, more strength, the ability to endure harder chemo. For fifteen years it was among the most widely prescribed cancer drugs in the United States, and by 1998 it had become J&J's most profitable product, more than a tenth of company sales.

The problem was what EPO did to tumors. Cancer researchers found that it supercharged their growth. Inside the oncology field, the drug earned the nickname "Miracle-Gro for cancer." Patients taking harsh chemotherapy to shrink their tumors were simultaneously being given a drug that fed them. AMGEN and Johnson & Johnson knew. Study after study finding excess deaths was quietly buried.

When German researcher Michael Henke published his trial anyway in 2003, it showed EPO increased cancer progression by 69% and death by 39%; a breast cancer trial found nearly three times the deaths among women receiving it. Paul Goldberg of *The Cancer Letter* then revealed that multiple other studies hidden by J&J and Amgen had found the same pattern.

The most damning detail may be this: while selling Procrit to cancer patients, J&J was secretly running a research program on an anti-EPO compound to shrink tumors. They were not confused about the mechanism, they just didn’t want the truth of it published. Even while researching for this section of the chapter, most of the articles or scientific journals about the EPO scandal are entirely paywalled. If I had never read that book, and perhaps if you had never read this one, it would remain quiet.

The FDA added a black-box warning in 2007 and restricted the drug's use in cancer. Harris estimates the death toll in the hundreds of thousands. By his account, it is roughly half a million. It is one of the largest cancer drug disasters in American history, and barely anyone knows about it.

What started as an effort to reduce the suffering of cancer patients led to their deaths, and then to the burial of the evidence. This is a chapter about how limitations reduce care, even in spite of our best efforts to build it.

Software, like everything else, is something with immense tradeoffs and limitations. This is the case even as our society becomes more digitally integrated. To build something effective, we need to know where the limits of that affectation are.

## Diffusions of effort.

It is very easy to say that this software project you built is tech for good. It is hard to empirically prove and sustain that. Tech for good has often been, like many non-profit or philanthropic ventures, a place where misdirection and co-opting has diverted the field from its original efforts.

Tech for good, like any other philosophy or social movement, is open to multiple different interpretations.

Sometimes, these interpretations can be outside of the original intentions and spirit of what the movement was. The myriad of interpretations is where this misdirection comes from. Some misdirection may happen from natural philosophical evolution, and others come from the societal benefits that come from being associated with “social good” in any capacity. There’s a line in the TV Comedy, Silicon Valley, which parodies the tech industry in the 2010’s. The scene is at a startup pitch competition, and every single startup comes up to pitch a completely nascent technology that has no impact on the average human. They of course, codify the pitch as something that will be world changing and inevitable.

Often, software and the VC funding that pushes it forth creates a showboating quality to most new products, making them splash loudly and coat themselves in a veneer of inevitability and mass benefit. The reality of how these products land in the world is of a much bitter veneer.

This is why empirical review should be the top-line standard to qualify what meets “tech for good”. What it truly means when people claim their product will benefit the world can mean anything, so much so that it has almost lost all meaning at all.

## Veiling Systemic Issues.

David Rieff’s book, “A Bed For The Night” discusses a lot of his own emotions about this as a war journalist. He discusses how often, humanitarianism in high conflict areas acted as a fig leaf of sorts for political intervention. How, by providing care, governments could delay implementing true fixes and intervention to the problem that necessitated care in the first place. Humanitarianism is meant to be an apolitical offering in some of the darkest places in our world. Yet overtime, humanitarianism became a reason to continue war, or to avoid political intervention entirely.

That line of logic often applies directly to tech for good. There are a number of societal problems that greatly benefit from a software product, and millions whose lives are better for the inventions that have come out of the field.

There is also an untold number of things that have delayed legitimate action into reforming systems. This is one of the core internal discontents at the heart of software of care. What can we do as inventors to provide relief, while also promoting longer term fixes that stop the need to provide care in the first place?

Supporting software of care on its face sounds very easy. It also is very easy to claim that an interface provides the solution necessary to address the problem at hand. This is even more prevalent in software of care because of the potential scale and access that technology provides. Often, the pressure to gain resources and status enables stretching of the truth and effectiveness. It is our job to understand the role our software products play in the system we plan to inhibit, where we can maneuver, and where we can reform pieces of the system outside of ourselves.

## Diluting, and diverging incentives.

There are often diverging incentives at the core of social good work. In forging forward to solve a problem entirely, we aim to essentially make ourselves a self-dissolving organization. Therefore, ensuring our success is ensuring the end of our work, the jobs of the people who may work with us, and the spot in the sunlight we may get from this work. This doesn’t apply to all solutions, especially ones that will be continuous, like disability, aging, unemployment, etc.

But, this does apply to solutions where the end goal is to solve a deeper problem. This includes homelessness, domestic violence, school shootings, etc. These are problems where the end goal is not just maintenance and mitigation of the problem, but ideally total eradication of the most harmful instances.

This can provide negative incentives within an organization, where they can confuse their organization’s survival with solving the greater problem. Entities can become embedded within an ecosystem, but can also hold it back due to rigid attitudes and assumptions that were correct years ago, but are no longer correct today.

They may look at a tool they have that is waning in impact and should be replaced, and do not consider doing it because of its history or centrality to the organization’s identity, despite its’ growing ineffectiveness. These ideas of preserving ineffective, yet pre-existing solutions and considering them good enough is another internal discontent. But, this provides a worthy challenge for any inventor who is able to recognize these solutions as lacking adaptability needed to survive in a coming wake of change.

In addition to corporate entities not wanting to forgo the cornerstones of their empire, financiers in many ways, shape the solutions we see today. A lot of social good programs are formed, not by what is most effective, but it most likely to pass through voters or through donors. The major tech companies we have today are not funded with due diligence, but capital that follows the promise of growth, no matter what road it may lead us down.

In this way, the incentives of what ‘gets funding’ often limits the solutions that can exist and scale.

## Confounding aspects of human behavior.

Unfortunately, as many humans know, we do not always do what is good for us. We innately imagine that if a piece of software existed that could help us or make our lifestyle easier, or smarter, that we would adopt it and tell everyone we know about it, and they would take full advantage of it. The truth is not that. Even when there are products, or tools that people can use, they may not take advantage of it. Not because the product is ineffective or bad, but just because humans do not always operate according to how we think they should.

Kentaro Toyama’s book, “Geek Heresy: How To Rescue Social Change From the Cult of Technology” addresses this directly as a core barrier to the tech-laden future we all seem to want. He’s coined the idea of the Law of Amplification: that technology amplifies all human behaviors to a more exaggerated extent. Later in the book, he comes to the conclusion that was is needed are not more technical interventions, but initiatives that create better forces within us and the people we serve.

## The artifice of good faith.

Often, when software doesn’t fulfill its’ potential or does something that harms users, it is often not because an entity or an inventor doesn’t have the knowledge to do better. Sometimes, they knew there was a chance that people could get hurt or otherwise the product could have inverse consequences, and they proceeded forwards. As Johnson and Johnson’s EPO scandal shows, it’s not about the fact that we don’t measure harm or don’t know our social good products don’t work: it’s that acknowledging and facing it is inconvenient. We would rather choose the comfort of our complacency instead of the work and pain that confronting harm and resolving it would engender upon us.

Humans see terrible things happen, and we cause terrible things to happen. Building a truly good product that impacts people is one goal that an operating entity may have out of many. Decisions that look evil or malicious can be chalked up and resolved to operating constraints, or just simply the lack of internal desire to do something about a problem that hurts people.

In this way, there is a limitation of good faith. People will build a product knowing it is not ready, not safe, or that it has an inverse effect, and it is often released anyways. In this sense, knowledge about operational procedures is not the bottleneck, it is the individuals and systems making and incentivizing the choices that go into developing and sustaining these products. That is why I have aimed to share and prove each thing to you, as opposed to believing you will take my word for it.

I do not want you to blindly follow all advice in this book, but to build your own knowledge that will lead you to build truly spectacular products. With that freedom of intellect comes the inevitable knowledge that you may read my warnings and ignore them. That is the cost of education: sometimes, it does not transfer as fully as intended.

In that vein, I have chosen specifically to write this book in a good faith lens as much as possible. But I want to delineate that it is a limitation that you will inevitably come to face, regardless of if you truly believe in this and follow it to the letter with vibrancy and faith. Apathy, lack of funding, lack of care, or just lack of ability to keep someone’s head above water are all factors that can contribute to structural weakening of products that may have a good impact, or structural strengthening of those that do harm. A lot of harm occurs not because someone didn’t know better, but just because it wasn’t incentivized to stop that harm from happening.

Working to build public social good software is one of the hardest things I have ever done, or could ever do. I assure you that you will also experience that. This is why preserving yourself is preserving the mission. Having passion and empathy for those that you serve, and a deep connection to what you want to do can be crucial for maintaining a connection that enables you to stay and serve in good faith.

## Structures we inherit.

The most frustrating of all, software can be limited by the structural forces around us. Even when we are able to design a solution that is an innovation in many ways, we often must work within the constraints of an industry that is not ready to meet the product we are ready to release. This was a strong, and common frustration while at Faura and during my time in the insurance industry. Often, a favorite conversation topic of insurtech founders at Leadenhall Market in London would be the frustration and black boxes of the institutions we are dealing with.

These structural limitations are not just frustrating, but often life-threatening. These are situations like health insurance denying life-changing coverage, non-profits not having enough funding for more beds in homeless shelters, or not having enough accessible mental health services in a given area. These are not situations solvable through more software. In fact, these limitations are sometimes roadblocks placed in front of care on purpose. Sometimes, the point of a system is the cruelty it enforces.

So, although you may build a software product that eases one burden of the system - another bottleneck downstream is what you are sending people towards.

Ultimately, this is why I left the mental health advocacy side of my work. And why after building 2, I hope to never build another mental health app again. I find it not only incredibly challenging engineering-wise, but also morale-wise to feel as though you are washing the lambs of your users to put them on a sacrificial table, not knowing what that sacrifice will spawn. The best way to engage with solving a system is to perhaps let it fail on its own.

## Limitations of care, itself.

Perhaps, the hardest limitation is the false idea that software can do it all, and by putting an interface to something we are thereby solving it. This is a discordance I faced often working on mental health apps. Although the interfaces were beautiful gradient blurs of lush support, underlying mental health issues in the United States have spiked. Mental health apps have been shown to not be greatly effective aside from mild reductions of depression and anxiety. Even then we don’t know how long those effects last.

The sheen of software is often what we want it to be, more than what it actually exists as. Software then, can be a placebo that pulls wool over the eyes of people who fund and potentially benefit from a solution. This is one of the major limitations we must contend with, especially as it is a major function of how software companies operate.

To hold someone going through a dark, deep descent is to realize how powerless you are. The truth is, software for the most part will not be a savior in the night coming to stop things. It will be a flashlight. Perhaps, an ineffective one at that. It will not stop the onslaught, and seemingly unending bad things that continue to grow and remain unabated in our world.

This itself is crushing. That engineering, thinking, logic, design, effort, and more can simply be nothing more than a crumple zone of a car. It is so frustrating, because these are some of the sharpest tools humanity has. Engineering and science make so much of our world more bearable, resilient, and safer. To realize your sharpest tool may never be enough is shattering, to say the least.

It is easy to desire to fix pain. It is brutal to be a witness to it.

It does not stop the crash. It softens it. Sometimes, softening an impact is how you can make sure people get out alive. I no longer think today, as I thought I would at 16, build the world’s most impactful tech non-profit. I will not build an app that solves suicidal thoughts, or that wipes the pain of assault away. I don’t think there is any such thing, although I hope more than ever that can be.

The things that we want to provide care towards, and remove from our user’s lives have existed for thousands of years, and they will continue to. Perhaps we are setting ourselves up for failure when we optimize for eradication. Putting these tormenting things in the dark forever feels like the more emotionally enriching option. It is too hard to live in a world and admit that we cannot eradicate the things that we see destroy us, and destroy others around us. But it is by admitting weakness that we build into our strengths.

I wonder a lot about the application I would build now for sexual assault and abuse survivors. How it would look a lot less like features that may push someone to gain the strength to report, and about how much more it would focus on just helping someone define what is up and down, left or right.

A true form of care is to accept the terror around us, and not let it paralyze us from action. Optimism is beautiful. But you cannot build a product around optimism that we completely stamp out assault. Or abuse. You could, but it would not be a product grounded in the reality of now. This is why you must understand the web before you become tangled in it. There is modifying the terrors, minimizing the chance they will happen, and teaching people about them. But there is no banishing them from our world forever.

We must design the future, and grow in our best shape towards it. However, we also must not lose sight of the system we have now: of what we have created and released into real hands, and what the circumstances facing those real hands are.

In ignoring our blindness, we can fail to rely on the other senses that may keep us tethered. This tethering is the core circulatory system that encodes software of care. Who truly is the user? Where are they in the world? How can we find them and offer care to them in a way they find enticing? How can they find and come to trust us? How can we maintain the fidelity of that trust through changing conditions?

This blinding opiate of the future is omnipresent among founders, futurists, and innovators who are often at the frontlines of inventing products. Yet, we need to learn to tame the future in order to get there. We must implement our laurels in the present, as much as we can. This is why I insist on a strong foundation of understanding the mechanics of care, and measuring the core of it. All care of scale should be based on a ground truth, not an ideal of philanthropy or politicization.

In setting our sights on the current day, we have a path to the future. In setting our sights on the future alone, we plot a course off the edge of an imagined world.
