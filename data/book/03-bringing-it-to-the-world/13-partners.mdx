You may imagine that the hardest part of building social good software is designing the tool itself: it’s actually the home that you give it that best sets it up for success. Software products are not agnostic of the environment that they are created in, they’re directly shaped by it. For some reason, myths abound of the product that is created, found and made viral, and then that continues to carry the product through the world. Although this can be true, it is by no means common, nor should you expect it.

The reality is that setting a product up for success means having a vast network of people: people who are reviewing it, volunteering for it, guiding it, otherwise critiquing it, or referring people to it through their own product.

Surrounding yourself with good partners and a great network is one reason why repeat founders have an advantage over first-time founders. Ignore the power of great (and bad) partners at your own peril.

## External Partners

### Users

Your partnership with your end user is possibly the most important relationship you will have.

Giving care through software implies that you will need to keep the relationship in a state where the end user feels comfortable enough to receive care. This is why you should go out of your way to make sure to invest into partnerships with your users. It looks like not only look like taking user feedback and providing them the product, but potentially creating spaces for them to share their inputs or frustrations on the products.

Managing, and continuing the cycle of communication about your product is one of the greatest sources of feedback and leverage you will have. Communication from your users determines your impact, growth, roadmap, and the future of your company. Taking it seriously and finding multiple ways for people to engage across your product or operating entity will help set you up for success in maintaining the longevity and value of that communication cycle.

- **Consumer Advisory Boards**: Consumer advisory boards are often volunteer boards composed of users who are the first to hear about, and shape new products or decisions. These boards act as a way to involve the voice of a user in a very direct way that can enable better decision making within the software process. You should enact caution when it comes to figuring out how to reward users, and manage the board.
- **Online Forums**: This is an easy one to get started, and a great one to have for organizations that have a more broad general pool of users. Online forums provide an easy way to centralize opinions and make users feel more involved.
- **Feedback Forums**: These can be also easy, and done programmatically. Think of all of the modals that you may see that ask, from a sad face to a happy face. This may also be a form on a website sourcing information from a user, to Instagram polls that ask users to help with design & feature decisions. Anything, whether large or small, that shows users also have a hand in the product can make them feel very appreciated.
- **Crowdsourced Feedback Tools**: Crowdsourced feedback tools can be polls within newsletters, a form that asks for feedback, a forum where users can upvote and comment on features they want to see.
- **Office Hours / Town Hall**: Office hours can be open time for your users to talk to you directly, through an in-person or online setup where you are able to interface with people in a live setting.

Partnerships with your users should be handled with care. Vulnerable users can often be worried about paternalistic and condescending care given from others, or can feel like their contributions are not valued or taken away from them.

### Stakeholders

Stakeholders are indirect relationships to the users. These are people and institutions that may be involved in regulating your product, being a liaison to the users, deciding funding for programs your users may utilize, et cetera.

- **Independent Oversight Boards**: This is a great option for those that have the resources, but can publicly become a big problem. Consider when Meta publicly diverged from their independent review board, and many considered that another instance of Meta acting against what users wanted to see. Independent Review Boards only work so well for partnerships as long as they remain empowered and autonomously are able to act. In the event that you have disputes with them, problems may arise.
- **Advisory boards**: If you have specific people from the domains that you act in that would provide relevant guidance, it would be beneficial to potentially create an advisory board. If you are a large enough entity, many smaller companies would like the chance to be on the advisory board to advance their resume, and would appreciate the additional PR for their organization.
- **Government Agencies**:
  - Government agencies and institutions may have a big role in accelerating, or decelerating products.
  - Faura enjoyed a fruitful relationship with Departments of Insurance, which are government entities in each state that regulate insurance companies. Because insurance is a legal requirement, governments work to make sure that insurance companies are counterbalanced. Our product aligned well with the push for consumer transparency and desire for homeowners to have more autonomy over their policies being non-renewed in high natural disaster risk areas.

### Other companies

As you define your role within the ecosystem, there will be companies that provide a natural compliment to your work. For example, at Faura we were able to develop a revenue share partnership with Thumbtack that gave us a percentage of jobs booked.

Partnering with others is something that you should develop organically over time. You should also do it as you start to understand the dynamics of the field, and keep those strongly in mind as you figure out who to trust and reach out to. The non-profit industry often can compete for market share, and products in the space may cause the appearance of straining funding or market share of “help”.

There are many people who are very good, and will be good stewards. There also are people who will be terrible, steal your work, or otherwise act against your interest while pretending they are not. This is a small (perhaps less than 1%) portion of people. But you will run into them, and if you desperately search for partners with open arms or distribution, you leave yourself vulnerable to being taken advantage of.

You should also work to clearly define your value proposition to other software products in the space. Perhaps, you have a public API that enables people to look up natural disaster relief grants. Otherwise, you might serve a niche segment of the population. But most of all, work to thoroughly understand the reasons why partners may wish to link with you, both on the negative side and positive.

Partnerships with other software companies can be life changing. They can also be a waste of time, and cause scope creep and additional business logic within your code that may contribute to tech debt over time. Do not be afraid to sunset, or fire bad partners. Partnerships with others can be a large moat, especially in legacy industries. They can also become a time suck when partners do not have strategic impetus to include you. Work to create and keep partnerships on a timeline as opposed to an indefinite engagement that will get pushed to the back of the task list.

Ideally, you should have a small number of potent partners. You also, for sustainability reasons, don’t want to over-index or leverage on any one software partner. You should, just like looking for change coming to your product, look for changes happening within the field that may open up new and more relevant opportunities for you.

### Interlopers.

There is often in social good projects a person who has a lot of ideas and passion for what you may do, and will very much make it visible to you. They will also perhaps not be within the core groups of users you want to serve, or will be far away from it.

Sometimes, they will suggest infeasible or just plain incorrect and bad ideas. However, they may have an outsized impact on your organization for one or more reasons: they are a potential or current funder, they have a large amount of connections in the space, etc.

You will need to learn to manage them to properly enable them to contribute to the organization, without letting their perspective dilute the product or greatly shape the approach of what you are doing. This will be hard, and requires a degree of personal touch and honesty. Navigating their advice with gratitude and learning how to further engage them in an instructive way may prove to be valuable.

The most important thing is that they have ideas that show they want to be helpful and beneficial to the organization, and working with them to redirect those ideas into productive contributions would be the beneficial move for both of you. There may also be times where this person in question only wants things their way, or the highway. In which case, it is up to you to decide how much you want to let this person wield influence.

## Internal Partners

### Cofounders.

A cofounder is a marriage of business partners. It is one of the most serious things you can undertake, and it will financially tie you together. Most advice relating to cofounders is not advice I personally agree with, as it posits it to be a process like finding someone to hire for a job. Especially for a co-inventor of a software product that impacts people, it will be crucial to find a co-founder who is able to navigate the space, has their own value system about it that aligns with yours, and understands the turbulent waters in which software for social good projects can operate.

I would advise that you should go to spaces where you are co-working with people and can see their output over a period of time. I also think for social good software, it is best to find people that have a personal connection to the mission, and personal goals aligned with those of the mission. Then, it’s prudent to make sure they are aligned on the entity that will own the product, and what they want from that entity.

Lastly, conflict resolution and knowing that they can act as an autonomous partner outside of you is the key. With a cofounder, you are looking for a twin or triplet who can own a specific segment of the project to share the load. Look for someone who is additive to your skills, but does not duplicate or subtract from them. In any co-founder, there needs to be a good balance of ownership, and also communication and humility. If you get hit by a bus, that’s the person who gets to drive what you have built together. Can you trust them to do that?

Even more so, the average business venture takes about 10 years. Most people will get the chance to have a few, true shots at building a product and an entity that can impact people. Do yourself being able to develop a relationship with this person over 10 possible years? Do you trust them, their judgment, and their skills enough to take one of the finite shots of your life in partnership with them?

This is why cofoundership is more like a marriage than hiring someone for a job. You are tied together legally, potentially in the eyes of your board, and at their mercy and actions. Only by the both of you pushing forward as much as you can, and turning towards each other instead of away, will you be able to make anything worthwhile for the group of you.

Cofounder matches should ideally cover 2 areas: a skills assessment, and a relational assessment. A value assessment is also a good idea, but may not need to be a blocker immediately. However, you should know how someone works, handles relationships, and wants to see their values in the business prior to bringing them on as a cofounder.

Your ideal cofounder will not only provide weight ahead of time through the skills they bring to the table, they will also have the relational skills to be able to navigate the lived realities of being a business partner. Often, most cofounder problems can be reduced to mismatch of skills, relational needs, or values regarding the business and how it is run. The hardest problem often to overcome are the relational aspects, and the value driven aspects. Skills can easily be sharpened and changed by someone willing, but how someone sees relationships within business and the values that they want in their life and company are often fixed objects that take a long, long time to change.

### Your team.

Your team, if you have one, will be crucial to consider. Often, burnout and mental health issues are rife in the social good space. This in turn can create a bit of a self-decaying process over time, despite the best intentions of the team.

First, the organization may be resource constrained. As such, there is often not a lot of money, and any opportunities are considered urgent, and needing to be focused on immediately. This gets passed directly as urgency to the team.

The team, in turn, starts creating lower quality work and feels the urgency as instability and stress. This results in people surrounding the team (like users, partners, etc) also perceiving the organization as chaotic, rude, or inconsiderate. This is a major problem with community-facing non-profits, especially with users taking burnout from the team as inconsiderate, uncaring, or cruel. This creates lack of trust from the user and other stakeholders, which goes back into making the team more burned out and feeling more unappreciated.

The solution of this is complex, but is in part clear prioritization and scope. It requires deft leadership and constant problem solving. Partially, this is why you need to keep your own head above water. If you cannot save yourself, you cannot save others. If you cannot save yourself, you will rely on others to carry you more and more. Temporarily needing to rely on others to push forward the product is not a problem, but being so burned out that your actions are stunting the team will cause the rose to wilt from the inside out.

This is one of my biggest regrets with my first non-profit. I was seeing my commitment to the organization instead of my own life as proof of how excellent of a founder I was. But in not focusing on my own life and not properly handling succession and decentralizing myself from the organization, I set up the entity that was the focus of my life to fade before my eyes. My commitment to the organization perhaps did not steady the anxieties of my team, but most likely amplified their concerns. An inventor who is both too intertwined, and not intertwined enough with the product are warning signs in their own way.

In this way, investing in your team and maintaining a positive relationship with them is an investment in your product, and the impact. A good team should carry you, and they often do. You should also carry them, and make sure to protect them from the forces of the world. The best thing you can do for your team is handle your role well, and to protect them to do good work as much as you can.

A team that is equipped to meet the challenge will create so much more benefit than you may ever as a solo inventor, and it’s through connecting with others that the product is able to take a prismatic view that builds on the strengths of many, instead of the weaknesses of a few.

A lot of my advice for cofounders will also apply to your team, just in a more diluted way.

### Your board.

If you are a non-profit, this will be one of your primary points of focus. Non-profit boards are expected to guide and oversee the stewardship of the organization, while also fundraising on behalf of the organization. For for-profits, they are most likely pre-existing shareholders or other key business partners who want to keep a close eye on things and to ensure proper stewardship of their capital or of the business.

In both cases, a common failure mode is a board that rubber-stamps everything, unconditionally. A board that approves everything and asks no uncomfortable questions is not governance, but is moreso corporate theater with a quorum. It can feel pleasant, especially for founders, and it fails exactly when you need it. A board is supposed to step in when you are too close to a decision, too exhausted to see a risk, or too attached to a product that is harming the people it claims to serve. If your board has never made you defend anything, you have an audience where you need oversight.

For-profit boards carry a second, structural problem: they can be entirely investor-aligned. A board of shareholders is doing its job, as the law defines that job, when it optimizes for the return on capital, and nothing in that job description mentions your users. Every pressure gets amplified in a boardroom where no seat answers to the people receiving care. This is not a reason to avoid investors. Instead, it is a reason to choose them the way you choose any partner, knowing they create the pressures you will face. This is another reason that you need early alignment and to make sure you know who you are letting into your company, and what they want out of it.

Ideally, fight to keep at least one independent seat held by someone whose credibility comes from the problem space rather than the cap table. The time to negotiate board composition is when they want you, as it is rarely that time ever again.

Non-profit boards fail differently, but rhyme similarly. Stacked with friends and family, they stay silent out of loyalty. When they are stacked purely with donors, they govern the fundraising and not the care. You want a mix that can actually interrogate and hold you accountable towards the work: someone who knows the problem space firsthand, someone with the professional skills you lack (legal, finance), and people who can genuinely raise money, with everyone clear on which hat they hold. Lived experience of the problem you serve belongs on the board itself, not just in an advisory committee that gets consulted after decisions are made.

As you hopefully know if you have a board, they engender specific legal protections and are often the only entity that can fire the CEO or executive director. A board is a sign of good corporate governance, and a solid system of checks and balances. When it goes awry, a board can silently watch as a product and company fails, or can drive decisions and thoughts that contribute to a bottoms-up decay.

### Advisors.

An advisor is like a lawyer. When shit hits the fan, if you have a bad one, you will pay for it. A good advisor is like a car: you should drive it prior to purchase, and make sure if it breaks down that you are not stranded. Advisors should be complimentary to your skills: if you are bad at sales, find someone who you can call with sales questions.

Don’t find people who just want to please you, or instill their own ideas.

You need people who can be honest with you. Engage your advisors in more parts of the entity than just you (if there is more than one of you). Enabling an advisor who is interested in the company to get a broad view of your entity and your product will make their advice more accurate, and get them embedded into the mission.

### You.

I am hopefully, perhaps naively, imagining that you are a person reading this and not a bot scrapping my knowledge for an AI model. You, as the creator of this software, may feel a quite unique sense of pressure. I think software of care can create and combine two uniquely stressful implications from the non-profit and startup field that can be visceral and mentally challenging.

This is the implication: Software is incredibly fast, and high-reaching. That, combined with the ease of access, can mean a mental firestorm of pressure. Both to release and continue releasing software, but also to make sure the software that you release doesn’t add more harm than it removes. You can fix a lot of problems, you can do it fast and far reaching, but you may accidentally fling poison further than you can cure it. This unique pressure, both of the ease of scaling and the degree to which it’s possible to hurt others, is unique to social good software. It also kept me up many nights personally.

Here I was, with my laptop, watching all of the pain of the world swirl through it but not shipping code to fix it. I had, through technology, access to the world’s pain and a way to solve it. When I went online, everyone was solving it, or getting funding for their company, and going viral. Why am I not solving cancer through agentic AI workflows? How did this 19 year old get 15 million dollars?

When I opened my email, I had people in the throes of life’s worst experiences begging me to figure out how to help them. I was not mentally prepared for what I was opening my life to, at all - and that’s an extreme understatement.

At 16, people would come up and tell me about the time they tried to kill themself, or they would email me for advice about getting raped. The urgency of wanting to solve this, and to feel like if I just worked a bit harder I could save more people, lead to me tunneling into deep burn out that I couldn’t even conceptualize. Everyone told me I was doing something amazing, and it could save the world. So, why couldn’t I save the fucking world?

I was so burned out after running Astra in deep poverty for 6 years, and was barely functioning. I ran the organization while working 2-3 other jobs at a time, using my salary to entirely fund the organization and paying enough myself to stay barely alive.

Although I knew it couldn’t be sustainable. I kept mentally convincing myself that I would come back to the organization, despite my assault, and that I had to be there for the people who needed me. It was, even through my darkest moments, the brightest light in my life - even as I was utterly failing to tend to it and watching it go entirely.

Ultimately, I shut it down after realizing that I was deluding myself and that it was triggering for me to work on. The true work was not in pushing myself harder to be there for others, but in learning how to build a life for myself and manage my own crises first. It sounds so mellow in written word, but in actuality, it was a violent transition.

Especially because I formed it in the wake of my own multiple suicide attempts, closing down Astra in 2024 felt like the oxygen was ripped from the air. A year later, I was still struggling. There was a particularly brutal panic attack in front of someone I was in love with, where I kept sobbing that without Astra, I didn’t deserve to be alive. Losing it was a necessary brutality, but a brutality nonetheless.

Multiple people told me that they were worried about my devotion to it and how it being the sole purpose of my life was concerning. In my living room, I had massive posters of all of Astra’s products that I sat and worked under daily. My license plate said ADASTR. I wore an Astra nameplate necklace for years, and I have star tattoos everywhere commemorating the organization. I built myself a tiny house in a trailer, so that I could live under the poverty line indefinitely to keep the organization running.

If love alone could have run that company, it would have never died.

Even now, thinking about shutting down Astra and not being able to overcome the struggle that drove me from it makes me ashamed. I did not ever wind down the organization correctly, and just let it silently die: that brings me a deep amount of shame, and guilt that I abandoned the users who gave me a purpose. I think about Astra every day, almost 2 years after shutting it down. I see it everywhere, and all of the time.

But, it needed to happen. I have now overcome the majority of my suicidal ideation, burnout, and other struggles that I was not facing, because work was a bandaid.

I have learned to disconnect from this as an adult with a frontal lobe, but growing up and starting my career in this world was very hard for me. We are often drawn to this work because of the trauma that we may have experienced that shows us how necessary it is: that trauma can tear us from the inside out, akin to swallowing magnets before going into an MRI appointment.

Even beyond trauma, if you have other mental health problems, this work can deeply exacerbate them and will push you to the limit. Not only have I seen this happen to me, but countless other founder friends who find the solution to their life’s woes in work. Every time, it’s a false idol. The repercussions are never as muted as you hope they would be.

I am grateful that I have found the time and the stability to have a therapist who has helped me work through this, and I am grateful that I have learned to be kinder to myself. But, I wish eternally that I learned that caring for others comes from allowing yourself also, to be cared for. Even if it’s only you caring for you. It becomes incredibly hard to disengage from the work that you may want to do, and there is a billion reasons not to. This means it is all the more imperative to do so.

The valleys of burnout and compassion fatigue are deep, and they take just as long to get out of as they did to get into. I would advise you if you’re managing this work to find immediate and tangible support, and to get a therapist who will help you set boundaries and disconnect from your work as you go. If therapy is inaccessible to you, please find a secondary outlet where you can vent about your work, and maintain friendships with people close to you. Your own continuity is the continuity of your product, and preserving your sanity is insurance for your invention.
